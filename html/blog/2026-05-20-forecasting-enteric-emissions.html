Introduction

PlanZero aspires to simulate the effects of strategies, in order to predict their likely impact on national emissions over the coming decades. The earlier post on modelling a Bovaer strategy for example, concluded that Bovaer could save about 9.5Mt {{CO2e|safe}} annually. As the saying goes though: "prediction is hard, especially about the future." For example, one key assumption supporting that conclusion was an assumption that the cattle population would remain constant in number and composition, and fixed at the average population in 2023. Historically the cattle population has fluctuated, arguably due to e.g. price and availability of feedstock, price and availability of land, domestic and international demand for beef and dairy products, drought, extreme temperatures, and even a recent innovation of "beef on dairy" operations enabled by new breeding technology. In the future, the cattle population will continue to fluctuate to some degree, due to at least those reasons. The result in the previous post did not include how much such fluctuations might affect the savings from Bovaer, this post brings estimates of uncertainty to the earlier conclusion that Bovaer could save up to 9.5 Mt{{CO2e|safe}} annually.

Also, going forward, PlanZero's analysis should always include estimates of uncertainty. This post brings a new guiding principle to the PlanZero's "About" page, to the effect that that PlanZero will strive to always include language and measures of uncertainty alongside all its narrative and visual predictions. The quote that "prediction is hard, especially about the future", is funny because it is superficially redundant, resonnant with common sense, and nontrivially, frustratingly true in statistical methods. Statistical methods can estimate uncertainties in various ways, but there is no "best method". Furthermore, all methods struggle to incorporate the infamous "unknown unknowns" that are nevertheless important for long-term corporate planning. I believe that the general difficulty of discussing differences in prior beliefs regarding the probability of things, compounded by the technical difficulty of estimating model uncertainty from data, amounts to one of the biggest obstacles to collective action to address climate change. I would like for PlanZero to take a best effort approach to estimating and communicating the uncertainty around its predictions. This post brings some updates to the simulation visualizations to show uncertainty estimates by default.

Uncertainty Quantification

Uncertainty Quantification is the science of quantitative characterization and estimation of uncertainties. There are multiple kinds of uncertainty in discussions of modelling, but before we introduce them, it is helpful to clarify what exactly is the phenomenon to be modelled: what are the uncertainties about? For PlanZero, at the highest level, the predictions are about the {{siteref("National Greenhouse Gas Inventory")|safe}}. Specifically though, the {{siteref("National Greenhouse Gas Inventory")|safe}} is published in the form of annual {{siteref("NIR", "National Inventory Reports")|safe}}, and immediately this presents some challenges. These challenges correspond to uncertainties. The types of uncertainty in the last column are explained below.

Emissions are largely driven by influences such as trade agreements, global market forces, population dynamics, innovation and technology adoption, land use, and an ever-changing climate itself. These drivers are all somewhat uncertain, especially further into the future. Significantly Aleatoric

Two broad kinds of uncertainty are distinguished in the quantification of uncertainty: Epistemic and Aleatoric uncertainty. In theory,

  • epistimic (or epistemological) uncertainty refers to a lack of knowledge or information about either the state of the world, or the processes at work in it; and
  • aleatoric uncertainty refers to the effect of randomness.
In practice, the distinction is only sometimes helpful. For an example where it is helpful, consider a situation in which we are missing key data that would inform the parameter of a model (such as, perhaps, the cost of on-site monitoring of Bovaer usage). In such a situation, it's natural to suppose the model parameter might be drawn from some distribution of plausible values and describe the resulting uncertainty in predictions as epistemic.

For an example of when the distinction is not very helpful, consider a situation in which data is to be explained, and two models are equally good at explaining results before 2022, but differ in what they predict beyond 2022. Then, if we considered that the models were accurate until 2022, we might think either model might the right one, and the uncertainty resulting from their diverging predictions was an epistemic one. On the other hand, if we considered that the models were both inaccurate up until 2022, we might be inclined to dismiss the data as random, and describe the uncertainty in predicting the future as aleatoric even though the models made different predictions for future years. Limited in utility though it may be, the terminology of epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty reinforces an important and inescapable point: that aleatoric uncertainty exists, and serves to constrain our predictions to be at least somewhat uncertain.

Probability Distributions and Alternate Narratives

Another two broad kinds of uncertainty are reflected in the use of probability distributions and narrative scenarios in planning. A common way to talk about probability distributions is in terms of confidence intervals. A confidence interval is an expression of uncertainty based on the shape of a probability distribution. When someone says "There is a 95% chance that a solar panel plant will sustain such-and-such production at such-and-such cost for between 20 and 30 years." they are expressing an interval (20-30 years) and a degree of confidence (95%), for a statistic (the working life of a solar panel plant) based on a belief regarding the probabilities of various true values for the statistic, which is called a probability distribution. Once a probability distribution has been estimated for a statistic, then other kinds of statements are possible too, beyond statements of confidence intervals: what is the most likely value of the statistic, the expected value, the probability that it turns out to be above or below some threshold, and so on. Probability distributions are most useful when

  • we are sure what variables the statistic depends on (e.g. labour and land costs, equipment type)
  • we are sure we have a complete picture of how the statistic might depends on these variables
  • we are unsure what value these variables [will] have, but we are sure we can assign probability distributions to them in a way that most people would agree with
because when all these conditions are all met, we can use computational and algebraic techniques to transform the input probability distributions into the output statistic's probability distribution. This enables lots of useful things: we can establish confidence intervals as mentioned above, and use the distribution to maybe minimize some risk/reward tradeoff depending on what the statistic is about. The reason for uncertainty in input variables may be either epistemic or aleatoric, it doesn't affect the methods for translating input uncertainty into output uncertainty. All that said, the three conditions are not all generally met, and in the more general case (of gross epistemic uncertainty) planners typically fall back on a very human way of dealing with uncertainty: narratives.

In the general case, when planners are unsure that they have a complete picture of how an output statistic might conceivably depend on input variables (in which case they are also generally unsure of the full set of input variables that may affect that statistic) then they typically fall back to a different style of thinking, which I'll call scenario narratives. For planning purposes, scenario narratives are typically a small number (usually 3 to 5) of carefully-chosen, internally consistent narratives about the future, which are chosen to open decion-makers' minds to the range of what could potentially unfold. For some examples related to our solar panel plant, we might use narrative scenarios to explore what might happen if a technology breakthrough enables a superior new type of panel after 5 years, or what might happen if the government changes the subsidy programs for solar project developers or large electricity consumers to use specific types of power generation technology, or the international flow of oil is disrupted by war or trade policy. Narrative scenarios are especially appropriate for exploring "black swan" events, but even the variety of events above (which have all happened within the last few years to some degree) is difficult to reflect in probability distributions. Narrative scenarios should be consistent with any and all data that is available, but they are relatively unconstrained in what they suppose may happen in the future, and in how they suppose the future world will work. Reasonable people will not agree on which scenarios are the most salient, or how to assign probabilities to them.

Uncertainty in PlanZero

PlanZero represents and sometimes quantifies uncertainty using multiple mechanisms. At the highest level of gross epistemic uncertainty, PlanZero represents narrative scenarios as simulations. Simulations, like narrative scenarios, are discrete, internally consistent statements about the future. When the set of {{siteref("Dynamic Element", "dynamic elements")|safe}} being simulated reflects the narrative scenario and corresponds to a {{siteref("NIR Model")|safe}}, then the result of simulation is a {{siteref("Scenario", "scenario")|safe}} (a set of {{siteref("Time Series", "time series")|safe}}) that corresponds so closely to the "narrative scenario" that I think it's appropriate they share the word "scenario" in their names. Different narrative scenarios should be created in PlanZero through different choices of {{siteref("Barrier")|safe}} elements. Scenario-based planning is supported in PlanZero by creating multiple models, each with a single strategy of interest, but each with a different set of barriers.

That said, the set of time series comprising a single PlanZero scenario are not constrained to represent just a single possible future. Among a PlanZero scenario's time series, there may be for example, the upper and lower bounds on the possible value of a statistic (such as the number of cattle); in this way, a single PlanZero scenario may represent the set of all possible futures that satisfy those bounds. Furthermore, the set of time series in a single PlanZero scenario may be organized in such a way that they reflect e.g. 1000 possible futures (or even 1000 parametrized sets of futures) based on different random numbers, drawn to reflect aleatoric uncertainty in some aspect of a model.

Considering the generality of what may, at least conceptually, be possible within a single PlanZero scenario, I should acknowledge that it is even possible to compose them. For example, it's possible to imagine a single PlanZero scenario that reflects a 50% probability that our solar panel plant faces competition from new technology after 5 years, a 25% chance that there's no new technology, but interest rates rise sharply after 3 years, and a 25% chance that some other narrative scenario unfolds. It is conceptually straightforward to weigh each of the {{siteref("KPI", "KPIs")|safe}} of each scenario by the corresponding probability of that scenario to compute weighted KPIs. Such a combination of possibilities by probabilities is called a {{siteref("Mixture Model", "mixture model")|safe}} in probabilistic modelling. This, finally, brings us to the technical innovation for PlanZero brought about by this post. This post brings support for mixture models to PlanZero Scenarios.

What's critical is to represent degrees of certainty and uncertainty. There is uncertainty, to various degrees, throughout these models:
  • statistics from historical years may be best understood as random variables with distributions, variance, bounds, and so on.
  • models relating statistics to emissions may be best understood as model families, whose parameters are not known with certainty
  • time series about which we have historical statistics may appear to evolve according to patterns; diverse models might fit the historical patterns similarly well, yet predict different futures
I would like for PlanZero to reflect all of these sources of uncertainty in modelling the future, so that where there is low uncertainty, there is a plausible and credible prediction that does not depend on whether the prediction is about something in the near or far future.
