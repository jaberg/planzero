Introduction

PlanZero has used many kinds of data to date, starting with the 2025 {{siteref("NIR")}}, but it has not employed probabilistic modelling. This post introduces probabilistic modelling techniques to PlanZero. Probabilistic modelling is good for reconciling multiple data sources, but this post starts with just one: NIR-2025. The post introduces two models — a straw-man "Constant" model, and a demonstrably and qualitatively superior 2-step autoregressive model — and shows how to compare them mathematically as predictors of the future. Probabilistic modelling is compatible with the scenario analysis used to evaluate strategies (like e.g. Bovaer adoption), and for strategies that are already active, inference algorithms can even assess whether strategies are working as intended (at least relative to data and a model); but illustrating how this can be done will wait for a future post.

A probabilistic model (also statistical model, stochastic model, or graphical model), can do any or all of the following things:

  • assess the probability of data (typically higher is better)
  • generate plausible data, by sampling
  • support infererence of meaningful latent variables, such as the parameters of appropriately-chosen probability distributions over oberved data
This post focuses on basic concepts of probabilistic modelling, applying all three capabilities above to the NIR-2025 data, and numerically assessing whether one model is a better predictor of the future than another model. It does this by first developing a very simple model. Even though this first model is essentially just mean and variance (Modelling NIR-2025 with Constants), it still goes through the motions of Bayesian modelling because those motions can be repeated to understand more elaborate models, such as the two-step autogressive model that's developed in the following section (Modelling NIR-2025 with an AR2 Process).

Motivation for Probabilic Modelling

A probabilistic model is a hypothesis about how some aspect of the world works, which makes predictions about some data that we have. Taking the NIR-2025 for example, as our data set, we might hypothesize (hypothesis 1) that for each

  • year, from 1990 to 2023,
  • province and territory (I'll call these just "territories"),
  • IPCC sector (I'll call these just "sectors"),
  • and greenhouse gas,
there is a quantity, in units of e.g. kt{{CO2e}} that has been estimated in support of Canada's reporting obligations under the Paris Agreement. Furtheremore, we might hypothesize (hypothesis 2) that the various national totals are sums of these territorial emissions.

Alas, neither of these hypotheses is strictly true. They are true for the majority of years, territories, sectors, and gases, but there are counter-examples to both. Counter to hypothesis 1, there are 81 records missing from the IPCC Sector relating to stationary combustion in the construction industry. Counter to hypothesis 2, estimated national emissions related to harvested wood products were sometimes only about 70% of the total (negative) emissions estimated by provinces, a difference exceeding 50 Mt{{CO2e}} (almost 10% of the national total) in some years. If we define discrepancy as the maximum difference over years and provinces, the biggest ones (over 1Mt{{CO2e}}) are listed in the following table. There are 11 records in the table, out of a total of 486 combinations of sector and GHG. Bayesian reasoning involves trading off two sensible but not-always-compatible ways to think: trusting your hypothesis about how you think things work (sometimes called your prior beliefs), and trusting your observations about what is actually happening. The data in the NIR-2025 is not meant to be interpreted too literally. In fact, NIR-2025 includes per-sector estimates of uncertainty on national totals for the base year (1990) and the most-recent year (2023). These uncertainties should be considered a lower boundon our uncertainty about these numbers. It can also happen, from time to time, that the protocols for estimating emissions are updated; this happened a few years ago in the area of vented {{CH4}}, which causes a re-assessment of the past estimates, and a change that went beyond the previously-estimated uncertainty. Maybe there really was a set of territorial estimates that added up the published national totals, it was just that one or more provinces and territories estimated the wrong values. Or maybe the provinces used more appropriate data and the national estimate was off. Or maybe they were both off. A probabilistic model together with a Bayesian inference algorithm can produce posterior estimates of what most-plausibly actually happened given the data, its uncertainty, and a conviction that territorial totals should add up to national ones.

Modelling NIR-2025 with Constants

One greatly-simplifying modelling hypothesis we could make is that sectoral emissions (per territory, per GHG) aren't actually changing at all over time, we just measure them in some way that makes it appear as if they are changing from year to year according to trends (but such trends are just in our imagination if they're anywhere at all). My goal with this section isn't to investigate a model that anyone in particular believes to be true, it's to go through the steps of modelling and analysis for a model that is relatively simple to illustrate, communicate, etc. We will see later, that this is a less-likely model than one that recognizes temporal trends. (Happily, this was also my intuition.)

Some background on probability notation is required, in order to express probability models.

  • {{latex("P(X=0)")}} means there's a number we don't know, we're calling it {{latex("X")}}, and this is the probability it is actually 0. {{latex("X")}} is called a random variable.
  • {{latex("P(X)")}} is the table (possibly infinitely large in theory) of all the values {{latex("X")}} might take, and their probabilities
  • {{latex("P(0)")}} is sloppy shorthand for {{latex("P(X=0)")}} when it's clear from surrounding context we're talking about {{latex("X")}}
  • {{latex("\\mathcal{N}(\\mu, \\sigma^2)")}} is a Normal distribution, which is non-negative function that sums (integrates) to 1. It is parameterized by two numbers: {{latex("\\mu")}} the mean, and {{latex("\\sigma^2")}} the variance.
  • {{latex("X ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(0, 1)")}} means {{latex("X")}} is a random variable, and its corresponding distribution {{latex("P(X)")}} is the thing to the right of the "{{latex("\sim")}}" symbol
  • {{latex("P(A=0, B=1)")}} means there's multiple numbers we don't know, and this is the probability that they both actually have these specific values.
  • {{latex("P(A=0| B=1)")}} is the probability that {{latex("A=0")}} if we make an additional assumption about {{latex("B")}}, namely that it is 1. If A and B are not independent, then knowing something about B tells us something about A; in other words, changes what would otherwise have been our {{latex("P(A)")}}.
Probabilistic modelling is a huge topic. I think it's typically introduced in secondary school, and covered in greater detail at various university levels, often with application to areas of science, engineering, medicine, social studies, mathematics etc. where probabilities are used. For more information and orientation, see e.g. wikipedia ( Statistical Model, Graphical Models), many tutorials online specific to probabilistic programming software packages (e.g. Stan, PyMC, Pyro; PlanZero is currently using NumPyro but that might change in the future) or a textbook such as Probabilistic Graphical Models (Koller and Friedman, 2009) for greater depth.

The following non-time-varying graphical model for NIR data will be our subject for the rest of this section.
{{latex("X_{(s,g,r,t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\mu_{(s,g, r)}, \\sigma_{(s,g, r, t)}^2)")}} Suppose that for each sector {{latex("s")}}, greenhouse gas {{latex("g")}}, region {{latex("r")}} (province or territory), and time {{latex("t")}} (annual total), suppose there's a number we don't know (random variable X) that's normally distributed about some regional mean {{latex("\\mu_{(s, g, r)}")}}, and with some variance {{latex("\\sigma^2_{(s, g, r, t)}")}}.
{{latex("Y_{(s,g,t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\sum_{r \in \\mathrm{regions}}\\mu_{(s,g, r)}, \\sigma_{(s,g)}^2)")}} Suppose further that for each sector {{latex("s")}}, greenhouse gas {{latex("g")}}, and time {{latex("t")}} (annual total), some number we don't know (random variable Y) is normally distributed about a mean that is the sum of the regional means, with a variance {{latex("\\sigma^2_{(s, g)}")}}.
{{latex("\\mu_{(s,g,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(0, 5)")}} Suppose the regional mean emissions are probably around 0, plus or minus 10. It is customary to scale data to be in this range before modelling them, so each emissions time series is scaled to have a mean squared value of 1. After this scaling, plus-or-minus 10 is a relatively un-opinionated prior as to what {{latex("\\mu")}} should be, relative to the NIR data, which will probably say that {{latex("\\mu")}} should be somewhere in the range of plus-or-minus 1.
{{latex("\\sigma_{(s,g,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{Exp}(1)")}} Suppose regional variances are probably tiny, but could potentially be somewhat large.
The prior densities for the {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}} terms are illustrated below. {{blog.figure_normal()|safe}}

Having made these suppositions above, and finally making one last pair, namely that {{latex("X = \\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, regional)}}")}} and {{latex("Y = \\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, national)}}")}}, we have ourselves a model of NIR-2025, and we can ask: what are {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}}? They are the valuable output of this modelling exercise after all, particularly {{latex("\\mu")}}, which is positioned as the true underlying emission rate for each sector, region, and gas, which adds up neatly to each national total. The Bayesian answer to this question is a joint probabilility distribution:

{{latex("P(\\mu, \\sigma|X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, regional)}}, Y=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, national)}})", "block")}}

This distribution is a called a posterior distribution because it's conditioning on our data. Generally, the posterior distribution of a graphical model's latent variables (variables that aren't directly observed, in this case {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}}) is not a distribution with a neat and tidy formula such as a normal or exponential. Even though, in general, we cannot write down a neat and tidy form for a posterior distribution, it is nevertheless often possible to work with these distributions algorithmically, via inference algorithms. It is, admittedly, possible to write down a neat and tidy formula for the latent variables in this particular graphical model, but I want to ignore that in order to demonstrate the more general technique of using inference algorithms. This first model was chosen for being simple rather than being good, and we'll need inference algorithms later when we're looking at another model that employs an autoregressive process. The next section uses an inference algorithm in the NumPyro software library to draw samples from {{latex("P(\\mu, \\sigma|X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, regional)}}, Y=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, national)}})")}} under our modelling assumptions.

Old text

In the course of trying to use the NIR-2025 for a few types of modelling (I hope some of which may be the basis for future posts) I observed something that caused me to pause and check some of my own assumptions about NIR data.

PlanZero first used the {{siteref("NIR")|safe}} in this post, and has dedicated several research efforts to understanding the drivers of emissions in each sector. The NIR includes provincial, territorialand national totals for each GHG, for each year. Since first exploring the NIR data, I had assumed (incorrectly, as it turns out) that

  1. When provincial and territorial totals are all present, they add up to national totals.
  2. When provincial and territorial totals (regional totals) are not all present, their subtotal is less than the national total, and the missing difference is the subtotal of the missing regional totals.
Qualitatively, both of these beliefs are usually true-ish. And it is true that sometimes data is censored by Statistics Canada when it would compromise some party's privacy, by e.g. reporting totals from a province with just a single cement plant, when individual plant data is protected by privacy legislation. However, quantitatively, neither is strictly true, at least in NIR-2025. There are examples of non-negligible differences between the sum of regional totals and the national totals, even when there is no missing data. There also are examples of the sum of regional total exceeding the national one. Admittedly, when I imagine how a complex national report such as the NIR comes together, this makes perfect sense; it would be extremely difficult to get the many agencies involved to agree on every number. However in terms of how a platonic ideal NIR would work, these discrepancies do not make sense; ideally, Canada's National total would be partitioned among provinces and territories, each of which could be associated with a specific number of kt{{CO2e}} for each IPCC sector, for each greenhouse gas, for each year since 1990.

This post is about a new bit of data processing in PlanZero, that estimates a platonic NIR-2025, which does all add up as it ideally should. It works by assuming the national totals are correct (this assumption can be relaxed in future work, but it's here for now), and estimating what the provincial totals most-probably were, such that they:

  1. are exactly their NIR-2025 values when the sum of regional totals matches the national one
  2. are relatively close to their NIR-2025 values for values that haven't been censored
  3. change smoothly from year to year
  4. always add up to the national totals
The statistical model used to create this platonic NIR-2025 could be called a Gaussian Process with Logistic (Softmax) output. The use of these models opens up several possibilities for future work, such as forecasting, recognizing uncertainty in the national totals themselves, and sampling from a distribution over plausible platonic NIR-2025s in order not to rely too heavily on any particular one. None of these capabilities is implemented yet, but they are benefits of Gaussian Process modelling of the kind presented in this post. More on this below in Next Steps.

A Platonic Ideal:

Predicting that the next few years will be similar to the most-recent few years is an approximation, and intuitively, probably a pretty good one. This post compares that approximation with another approximation, which is that the next few years is a linear function of a bit more of the most recent past, not just the very last year. Comparing predictive models isn't something we've done before on PlanZero, so I'll explain one way to do it. Train, validation, test for time series. That's the focus of this post. The NIR is a set of time series Canadian annual totals Provincial annual totals (which add up to the national total) The simplest thing we can do, to predict the future NIR, is an auto-regressive model. A windowed linear model should be demonstrably better than a moving average, because most sectors tend to move smoothly, they look temporally correlated. Going forward, by incorporating features and modelling relationships, we should see prediction error go down. Conclusion

Future work will look at splitting this model out into a model that includes drivers and emission factors, following e.g. the research from the posts. What happens if we keep re-applying the model to its own output into the future? Probably it gets worse, but worse than that, we also get worse at saying how much worse it is. For the more-distant future, different techniques based on bounds and narratives are more useful. It is possible, and critically important, to combine short-term statistical modelling with longer-term planning techniques, but in the interest of introducing one thing at a time, this post introduces simple prediction models in order to focus on the question of how we evaluate them. For the more-distant future, different techniques are more useful. One technique I intend to introduce in a future post is to bound the upper and lower future values of drivers, and the rates at which they can change, based on narratives. Narrative scenarios, such as "what if climate change leads to the expansion of productive farmland in Canada?" are not statistical models, but they are important planning tools that can be incorporated into PlanZero. Narrative scenarios can interact with short term statistical models when looking at the effects of strategies. A strategy such as "provide a buyer incentive for the purchase of EVs" connects to transportation emissions via beliefs about a causal link. We may have beliefs about the relationship between the amount of incentive, and the number of new EVs purchased, and about the relationship between the number of new EVs purchased and the amount of gasoline combusted in the transportation sector. Those beliefs can be translated to short- and long-term expected emissions impacts (we can simulate what would happen if our beliefs turn out to be true); but critically, those beliefs can also be tested (e.g. we can compare how many EVs were actually purchased with what we believed might happen). We will come back to this combination of narrative scenarios and statistical predictions in future posts. It is critical to combine them, so that we can use new information gained each year to inform our planning for the future.

Until next time,
