Introduction

This post takes a step back, reviews what PlanZero is trying to accomplish, recognizes the contributors that are helping to bring it together, and articulates a commitment I'd like to make to readers of the PlanZero site. I commit to leave posts alone after they're published, so if you read one, it will still be there later. Of course, iteratively improving all aspects of the site sometimes requires... slightly changing some of the posts. Read on if you're interested in how I intend to balance this commitment and requirement. With the publication of this post I'm also adding much of its content to the About page.

This post also includes some author guidelines. Posts on PlanZero have only one author so far, but I hope some day there may be others. This post brings some developer / contributor documentation to help start a discussion with potential new contributors.

About this project

PlanZero is an independent research project to model how Canada might achieve zero net emissions. Its mission is to quantify the potential impact of strategies — such as the research and development of new technologies, the scaling of new products, and the introduction of public policies — by situating them in long-term scenarios based on projections of Canada's possible future climate, population, energy demands, and other macro trends that might influence future emissions. It is motivated by belief that promising strategies exist, that they can be implemented in Canada and elsewhere, and that global net emissions can drop to net-zero over the decades ahead through the implementation of strategies such as those modelled in PlanZero.

There have been, and thankfully continue to be, many efforts to predict future climate, rates of emission, technology and policy impacts. As I build up a bibliography (barely started as of April 2026) this PlanZero site will situate itself in the context of ongoing and previous work. I believe that PlanZero is differentiated from other efforts in (a) focusing on Canada, and (b) in being structured as an open-source software project. As the project matures in depth and breadth, I hope it becomes a resource that can help shape public policy, and guide capital investment into research and technology development. I also hope that it may engage the community of Canadian students at undergraduate and graduate levels:

  • to help some learn about the diverse challenges of transitioning to a net-zero economy
  • to help some build compelling models of strategies that can and should be put into action
  • to help some learn about participating in distributed open projects
  • to help some learn about software engineering, architecture, and statistical modelling
A net-zero transition will take decades. PlanZero aims to offer opportunities for tomorrow's leaders to develop their skills and experience.

What to Expect from Posts: Stability

I think of the PlanZero project has having the following facets:

  1. posts - a narrative history of the development of the PlanZero site
  2. analysis - the PlanZero modelling and research pages
  3. about - project overview, direction, acknowledgements
  4. code ({{siteref("GitHub")|safe}}) - the state and history of all data, code, and content, in {{siteref("Git", "git")|safe}}
  5. {{siteref("GitHub Issue", "issues")|safe}} (GitHub) - a set of likely next steps, a rough roadmap
Posts, such as this one, provide an ongoing narrative history of the project. Posts are the things that I send out to people for feedback and general engagement. I would like to encourage people to forward posts to others. I would also like to act on feedback without having to ask "which version of the post were you looking at?" For these communications to be effective, posts on the site cannot materially change after they're published. For example, if someone sends a post link to someone else, the link should (a) still work at a later date when the recipient follows the link and (b) have the intended content when the recipient views the post. I hereby commit to developing this site in such a way that post links work in this way. I want to respect your time and effort, dear reader; after you have taken the time to read and undestand a post, I don't want to confuse you by changing its content after you've looked away.

For clarity, please do not expect stability from any of the other four facets of the project. The nature of the models and analysis may change, and the way pages on this server map to strategies and simulations etc. may be reorganized from time to time. At some point I may reorganize the site to e.g. no longer host the IPCC-Sector pages as top-level tabs, or turn what's now the "About" page into a multi-paged section. I may reorganize PlanZero's code so that files are moved to different places and mean different things. Finally, to round out the list, GitHub issues may be repurposed (especially the issues that I both create and then address). That said, throughout any and all of these more-or-less-likely changes, I commit to leave posts at their present urls and with their present content. Once a post is published, it will remain essentially unchanged — with one important caveat discussed in the next section: "Draft" status.

Post Stability Caveat: Draft Status

In the interest of granting myself some flexibility to change recently-published posts in response to feedback, I have introduced a mechanism whereby a post can have a status of "Draft". If a post is a draft, then

  • The word "DRAFT" appears in large letters in the title, wherever it appears.
  • The text of the post is subject to change without notice.
  • The entire post may be hidden, withdrawn, removed, etc.
This contrasts with the general rule of post stability. If a post is not a draft, then
  • No such word such as "DRAFT" appears in the title.
  • It should never be materially changed.
  • If it is superceded, it should be amended to include a link to the new post introducing the superceding content.

My typical workflow is to publish new material as a draft status post, send it to fellow collaborators, incorporate their feedback, and then remove the draft status. Moving a post from draft to non-draft status will typically the occasion for broader communication. In future a post leaving draft status may somewhat automatically lead to broadcast on e.g. mailing list, substack, X (twitter), slackbot, facebook, LinkedIn, and/or other ways of getting messages out, but I haven't decided how to handle that yet. For now I'll transmit posts manually, in an ad-hoc manner.

Please Don't Take Post Dates Too Literally

Perhaps it's a minor point, but for sake of clarity: posts have dates, and those dates are approximate. The dates of posts are chosen to create a coherent narrative, and reflect only the approximate time of writing. The date on a post does not necessarily reflect the time the work was started, completed, or last edited. When a non-draft post is amended to include a link to a superceding post, its own date will not change, but the amendment will mention a date e.g. "(Edited April 25, 2026: new post X covers this topic differently...)" but this date too can be approximate.

Authorship

You may have noticed that PlanZero posts are signed. Posts have authors and the authors' name(s) appear on the post. So far I've been a solo author, however I've had help from all of the contributors mentioned above. I don't think there's a comprehensive way to express who should be included among authors of a post, but as a rule of thumb to start, the authors of a post are the people who can answer to the questions "Why did you write that post?" and "Why did it say what it said?" That said, I will always strive to give credit where credit is due. PlanZero hasn't yet benefited from funding or sponsorship, but if that happens, I will acknowledge it publicly.

Drafting Guidance for Posts

This section is a (long) collection of notes on how to contribute to PlanZero as an author. If you have no such ambitions, feel free to skip ahead to the conclusion. This section contains a bit of background on open source and version control, and some guidance on the purpose of posts from a development perspective.

PlanZero is an Open-Source Project

Open-source software can be defined in a few ways, but I think of it as software whose source code can be read, modified, and extended by its users. Open-source software has existed in some form for as long as there has been software, and modern open-source projects include some of the most complex and stable software in the world, such as git itself, the Linux operating system, simulation tools for mathematical modelling, and many models used for atmospheric, ocean, and climate science. I have made PlanZero an open source project for three reasons:

  1. because I believe that that it will require the support, at some point, of an open-source community
  2. because I believe open-source community projects are relatively democratic, and robust to lobbying influence
  3. because contention in climate policy conversations may be better addressed by open-source modelling software, which is maximally transparent.
I have given it a BSD-3 license because it is is popular and reasonable license. If anyone wishes to use PlanZero under a different license, please bring it up as a GitHub Issue. Anyone with a GitHub account can draft a post, and open a "Pull Request" to request that the post be included on the site. This brings us to Version Control with Git, the topic of the next section.

Version Control with Git

Version control, and git in particular, has at least revolutionized, if not fundamentally enabled, modern open-source software. PlanZero is managed with git. Version control (or sometimes "revision control") software helps developers with two tasks that can otherwise be overwhelmingly difficult:

  1. manage versions of the full set of files making up a software project
  2. merge two versions together in a way that makes sense
Software developers are not the only ones to face this problem; anyone who has attempted to write a report collaboratively with a traditional word processor (not a real-time collaborative editor such as Google Docs) will have run into it. {{siteref("Git")|safe}} is a system for version control of projects of multiple files, which works best for plain-text files (e.g. programming code and html, not e.g. Microsoft docx). Git helps developers by tracking, on each developer's computer, which lines of text changed in which files, which files were renamed, which ones deleted, and so on. When it's time to merge changes from another developer, git can work through both devopers' histories nearly-automatically to produce a combined result. Git only requires guidance to handle the situation of two developers changing the exact same lines in different ways.

I believe that the success of version control software arguably created open-source communities. Version control software made it so easy to merge changes, that loosely coordinated developers, working on miscellaneous changes for any number of reasons, take the trouble to contribute their changes back to a common project. Perhaps sometimes it's altruism, and sometimes it's wanting to engage with an existing community. But I also believe on a practical level, it's sometimes simply easier to merge customizations to some main project (and share them), than it is to repeatedly re-apply customizations (or worse, re-develop them) every time the project released a new version. Whatever the reasons, version control is now a foundational aspect of software development (even among tight-knit development teams), and the culture of development with version control has shaped what I think of as the purpose of posts in PlanZero (next section).

Each versioned of set of files in git is associated with a {{siteref("Git Commit", "commit")|safe}} — a snapshot of the state of things and a brief message about it. Behind the scenes, when you make a commit, the git software stores changes relative to another commit, called a parent. Most git commits have a single parent, and the lineage of such commits is called a {{siteref("Git Branch", "branch")|safe}}. Merging versions is accomplished by merging one branch into another with a {{siteref("Git Merge", "git merge")|safe}}, which produces a special merge commit with two parents (mixing metaphors, arguably). devdojo has produced a helpful picture to explain:

A directory on a computer whose files are tracked by git is called a git repository. A repository on the GitHub website is associated with either a GitHub project, or a fork of a project. You, dear reader, are welcome to fork the PlanZero project. You may modify it, use it for whatever you like under the BSD-3 license, and optionally submit changes back to PlanZero. There are many documentation and tutorial sites online about how to use git effectively, and how to use GitHub specifically (as PlanZero does). To anticipate the point of the next section: in terms of this version-control discussion, the purpose of posts is to explain how merged development branches have changed (and improved) the project.

The Purpose of Posts

The development pattern of iteratively patching up and extending open source software applies at two conceptual levels with regards to PlanZero. At a lower level, PlanZero uses git via GitHub in the conventional way with commits, branches, merges, and so on, and the changes are described by git commit messages. PlanZero's git history includes a sort of narrative of commit messages, but these messages are small, text-only, and not written for the audience of the site itself. At a higher level, PlanZero uses posts to mark significant changes to the underlying project code, including the structure, analysis, and visualizations on the site. At this higher level, the site is like the "coherent, improved software system" with an ever-expanding set of models, visualizations, analyses etc. that nevertheless remain internally consistent. The posts narrate the incremental changes leading from the start of the project to the current state. The purpose of posts is to document, for readers of the site, why, and how, something on this site has changed.

A corollary to this bit of guidance, is that if there is no visible change to the site other than the post itself, then there is nothing beyond the post itself in need of explanation, and perhaps there should be no post at all. For example, a communication of the form "Group of people X should do Y instead of Z" should generally not be a post on PlanZero (unless e.g. the group of people is PlanZero contributors and the post is about a change to the About page). The closest message might be something like "Because of some new modelling or data added to PlanZero, we see that if group of people X did Y instead of Z, it would change such-and-such impact to the world from A to B." In the future there may be a "Perspectives" or "Recommendations" section with examples of using PlanZero to weigh in on public debate, but there is not yet such a section. There may be development from time to time that isn't reader-oriented, such as performance improvements, changes to how PlanZero is deployed, and additional tests, or governance discussion / documentation (such as this very post), but my personal wish is to tie such things to user-facing improvements as much as reasonable, in order to focus development efforts on things that provide benefit to users of the site. As written above, the mission of PlanZero is to quantify the potential impact of strategies — such as public and private research and development efforts, and public policies — and situate them in comprehensive long-term scenarios based on projections of Canada's possible future climate, population, energy supply, and other macro trends that might influence future emissions. Every PlanZero post should help PlanZero achieve its mission.

Conclusion

This post has brought a rewrite of the PlanZero About page, with a more concise vision and mission, a recognition of contributors, and some guiding principles regarding posts. The updated mission is to quantify the potential impact of strategies — such as public and private research and development efforts, and public policies — by situating them in comprehensive long-term scenarios based on projections of Canada's possible future climate, population, energy demands, and other macro trends that might influence future emissions. As the project matures in depth and breadth, I hope it shapes public policy, and guides capital investment into research and technology development. Meanwhile, there are about 100 universities in Canada, and if a few students at each one took an interest in PlanZero, they would make up a thriving contributor community. They are the next generation of shapers-of-public-policy and allocators-of-capital-investments. The guidance regarding posts is the beginning of a how-to-contribute onboarding document written with this population in mind.

PlanZero's next post will introduce a glossary. While a bibliography is on the horizon, a glossary represents a step forward: a place to consolidate the names of major sources, and an implementation of a cross-referencing mechanism for html-authored content. Relative to PlanZero's mission, the glossary helps quantify the potential impact of strategies by explaining how PlanZero models work.

Until then,

- James Bergstra
