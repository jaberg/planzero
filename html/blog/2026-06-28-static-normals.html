Probabilistic Modelling Notation

Some probability notation is conventional, in order to express probabilistic models. It builds on the introduction to probability in Appendix 1 of the Probabilistic NIR-2025 post. Probabilistic modelling is a huge topic. Basic concepts of probability are introduced across many years of primary and secondary schooling, and covered in greater detail at university levels. For more information and orientation, see e.g. wikipedia (Statistical Model, Graphical Models), many tutorials online that are specific to probabilistic programming software packages (e.g. Stan, PyMC, Pyro; PlanZero currently uses NumPyro) or a textbook such as Probabilistic Graphical Models (Koller and Friedman, 2009) for greater depth.

The following modelling notation used in this post to describe the Static Normals model and introduce Bayesian inference in Appendix 4: Inference in the Static Normals Model.

  • {{latex("P(X=0)")}} means there's a number we don't know, we're calling it an {{latex("X")}}, and this is the probability it is actually 0. {{latex("X")}} is called an {{ siteref("Unknown_Variable", "unknown variable")|safe}} in PlanZero, although many references refer to such a variable as a random variable.
  • {{latex("P(X)")}} is, intuitively, the table mapping all the values {{latex("X")}} might take to their probabilities. The probabilities add up to 1 to represent the fact that {{latex("X")}} must take one of them. The table might be infinitely large (e.g. a probability for each integer). Although it can be an abuse of notation, I'll also sometimes write {{latex("P(X)")}} to refer to a function defining the density of probability mass when the possibilities for {{latex("X")}} are a continuous domain of values, such as the real numbers.
  • {{latex("P(0)")}} is shorthand for {{latex("P(X=0)")}} when it's clear from surrounding context we're talking about {{latex("X")}}
  • {{latex("\\mathcal{N}(\\mu, \\sigma^2)")}} is a Normal distribution, which is non-negative function that sums (integrates) to 1. It is parameterized by two numbers: {{latex("\\mu")}} the mean, and {{latex("\\sigma^2")}} the variance. It's conventional to write {{latex("\\sigma^2")}} as the argument to the normal distribution because {{latex("\\sigma")}} itself is qualitatively the "width" of the distribution and only positive-valued variances are valid. Normal distributions are widely used in probabilistic modelling. You can read more about them on wikipedia, and see its shape in the figure below. (Sometimes these are called Gaussians but I'm going to call them Normals to be consistent with the probabilistic programming library I'm using.)
  • {{latex("\\mathrm{Lognormal}(\\mu, \\sigma^2)")}} is a log-normal distribution, a distribution over positive-valued real numbers with a given mean and relative uncertainty. There's an example below illustrating one possible shape of log-normal distribution, with {{latex("\\mu=-1")}} and {{latex("\\sigma^2=.7")}}. Note that unlike the regular normal, {{latex("\\mu")}} being negative doesn't make the distribution negative, it squeezes the distribution leftward toward 0. The {{latex("\\sigma^2")}} parameter doesn't exactly modulate the width, as it does for the normal distribution, but it reflects relative uncertainty, and it does affect both the shape and the mean of the distribution.
  • {{latex("X ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(0, 1)")}} means (1) that {{latex("X")}} is an unknown variable in some probabilistic model, and (2) that its probability distribution (or density) {{latex("P(X)")}} is the thing to the right of the "{{latex("\\sim")}}" symbol. In this example, {{latex("X")}} is distributed according to a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1.
  • {{latex("P(A=0, B=1)")}} features comma-separated terms. The equals-signs mean the terms are criteria. {{latex("P(A=0, B=1)")}} means the probability that unknown variable {{latex("A")}} is 0 and unknown variable {{latex("B")}} is 1.
  • {{latex("P(A, B)")}} also features comma-separated terms, but the terms aren't predicates with equals-signs. This is the notation for the probability table (or function, as appropriate to the domains of {{latex("A")}} and {{latex("B")}}) that maps every possible value of {{latex("A")}} and every possible value of {{latex("B")}} given that value of {{latex("A")}}, to the probability of them both taking those respective values. This sort of probability distribution is called a {{siteref("Joint_Distribution", "joint distribution")|safe}} because it is a distribution over multiple unknown variables.
  • {{latex("P(A=0| B=1)")}} uses the | symbol to mean the probability that {{latex("A=0")}} if we make an additional assumption about {{latex("B")}}, namely that it is 1. {{latex("P(A|B=1)")}} is called a {{siteref("Conditional_Distribution", "conditional distribution")}} because it defines a probability distribution for {{latex("A")|safe}} that is conditional on additional assumptions / criteria. A probabilistic model that includes {{latex("A")}} and {{latex("B")}} defines either explicitly or implicitly how they can be related by conditional distributions. Joint distributions such as {{latex("P(A, B)")}} can always be written as products involving conditionals {{latex("P(A, B) = P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A)")}}, and sometimes, such as when deriving techniques for Bayesian inference, such rewriting is very helpful.

Model Description

PlanZero's "Static Normals" model of the 2025 National Inventory Report (NIR-2025, or just NIR in this post) is that for each ...

  • IPCC sector (I'll call these "sectors")
  • greenhouse gas (sometimes just "gas")
  • province and territory (I'll call these "regions")
  • and year (from 1990 to 2023)
... there is a quantity of emitted gas in units of e.g. kt{{CO2e}} that has been estimated in support of Canada's reporting obligations under the Paris Agreement. Furthermore, there is a quantity of emissions for the whole country, which is the sum of emissions from the provinces and territories.

Finally, and most radically, the model is called "static normals" because it assumes that these quantities of emissions don't change over time. Instead, it assumes that the different estimates of emissions from year to year in the NIR are the results of completely random fluctuations, totally unforseeable and outside of anyone's control, which will continue indefinitely into the future following the same Normal distribution of annual emissions as has been observed historically. This is a unrealistic assumption because in many sectors, the reported emissions are defined in the NIR (following IPCC Guidelines) as being proportional to e.g., the number of cars, or trucks, or heads of cattle, which are highly correlated from year to year, and which follow long-term trends. Yet, there's no rule against using unrealistic assumptions in models. The consequences of making unrealistic assumptions in models are uncertainty and/or inaccuracy. We'll use this model to establish a baseline for uncertainty and inaccuracy in prediction, which can serve as a benchmark for future models aiming for greater certainty and accuracy on the basis of e.g. greater realism.

Formally, the Static Normals model is the following set of distributions over unknown quantities:
Unknown Variable and Distribution Description
{{latex("\\mu_{(s,g,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(0, 1)")}} A-priori, after emissions are scaled for each sector ({{latex("s")}}) and gas ({{latex("g")}}) so that 0 means 0 and 1 means the maximum absolute value of national emissions for that sector and gas over time ({{latex("\\alpha_{(s, g)} = \\max_{t}|m_{(s,g,t)}|")}}), the scaled emissions can be expected to be around {{latex("0\\pm 1")}}. The Static Normals model approximates this with a normal distribution and calls the unknown, scaled time-invariant emissions amount for each sector, gas, region combination {{latex("\\mu_{(s, g, r)}")}}. This and other a-priori distributions are designed to be refined by inference as explained in Appendix 4: Inference.
{{latex("\\sigma_{(s,g,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathrm{Lognormal}(-1, 0.7)")}} A-priori, the variance in regional emissions (looking across years) is not expected to be super-tiny, nor much larger than 1.
{{latex("\\sigma_{(s,g)} ~\\sim~ \\mathrm{Lognormal}(-1, 0.7)")}} A-priori, the variance national emissions (looking across years) is not expected to be super-tiny, nor much larger than 1.
{{latex("X_{(s,g,r,t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\alpha_{(s, g)} \\mu_{(s,g, r)}, \\alpha_{(s, g)}^2 \\sigma_{(s,g, r)}^2)")}} The model-estimated NIR emissions for each sector ({{latex("s")}}), gas ({{latex("g")}}), region ({{latex("r")}}, province or territory), and annual time period ({{latex("t")}}), are unknown variables ({{latex("X_{(s,g,r,t)}")|safe}}) whose probability is normally distributed about mean {{latex("\\mu_{(s, g, r)}")}} with variance {{latex("\\sigma^2_{(s, g, r)}")}}. Note that the distribution is the same for all annual time periods (giving the model its name "Static Normals").
{{latex("Y_{(s,g,t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\sum_{r \\in \\mathrm{regions}}\\alpha_{(s, g)} \\mu_{(s,g, r)}, \\alpha_{(s, g)}^2 \\sigma_{(s,g)}^2)")}} The model-estimated national-total emissions for each sector ({{latex("s")}}), gas ({{latex("g")}}), and annual time period ({{latex("t")}}) are unknown variables ({{latex("Y_{(s,g,t)}")|safe}}) whose probability is centred at the sum of regional means, with separate national variance. The distribution is the same for all annual time periods.

Background in Bayesian Inference

{{siteref("Inference Algorithm", "Bayesian inference")|safe}} in probabilistic models is about redistributing probability for unknown variables in order to balance two competing objectives: (1) to remain probable according to the a-priori distributions, and yet (2) to explain observations. The Static Normals model described in Appendix 2: Model Description includes a-priori probability distributions over {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}} that induce distributions over {{latex("X")}} and {{latex("Y")}}, which are meant to estimate quantities in an NIR. Yet we can observe the NIR-2025, in terms of either raw data, or the distributions developed in PlanZero's NIR-2025 model (see Probabilistic NIR-2025 post for details). [Bayesian] Inference algorithms can answer the question: What are the conditional distributions over {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}} given certain observable values for {{latex("X")}} and {{latex("Y")}}? This specific kind of {{siteref("Conditional_Distribution", "conditional distribution")}}, being a distribution conditioned on data, is called a {{siteref("Posterior_Distribution", "posterior distribution")}}. The unobserved variables in the model are called {{siteref("Latent_Variable", "latent variables")}}, and so a posterior distribution is a conditional distribution over the latent variables.

If only some of the observable data is used for conditioning, then the remaining data can be used to evaluate a model's predictions. For example, if we only condition on emissions data up to 2022, then variables related to year 2023 will be latent ones and inference will assign them posterior distributions. These posterior distributions can be interpreted as the model's predictions for 2023. Appendix 4: Pre-NIR-2026-m06, a NIR Prediction Challenge evaluates such predictions from the Static Normals model, using the method of inference discussed here in this Appendix.

Inference algorithms work on the basis of Bayes' Theorem. In standard probability notation, for any pair of unknown variables {{latex("A")}} and {{latex("B")}}, Bayes' theorem looks like:

{{latex( "P(A|B) = \\frac{P(A, B)}{P(B)} = \\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}= \\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{\\int_a P(B|A=a)P(A=a)}", "block")}}

Bayes' theorem shows how to relate a conditional distribution of the form {{latex("P(A|B)")}} to conditional distributions the other way around: {{latex("P(B|A)")}} and {{latex("P(A)")}}. Bayes' theorem is critical to inference in the Static Normals model because

  • in order to make predictions, the Static Normals model was defined with the conditionals one way, namely {{latex("P(X,Y|\\mu, \\sigma)")}}
  • to observe NIR data, we need the opposite conditional distribution {{latex("P(\\mu, \\sigma|X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(regional~totals)}},~Y = \\mathrm{NIR_{(national~totals)}})")}}

I don't know if it's possible to write down a formula for the posterior distribution {{latex("P(\\mu, \\sigma|X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(regional~totals)}},~Y = \\mathrm{NIR_{(national~totals)}})")}}. For some models, there are closed forms for some posterior distributions, but it's not always possible. What's often practical, is the use of Monte Carlo (random) estimators to compute samples of points from the desired distribution, based only on the conditional distributions used to define the model. Even with a Ph.D. in machine learning, I still find these algorithms amazing (read up on e.g. MCMC algorithms or stochastic variational inference if you're interested in how these algorithms work). This intersection of statistics, machine learning, and computer science is still the subject of active research, but the core capability of sampling from posterior distributions is relatively mature and for the purposes of this post we'll take it as given.

Probabilistic programming systems enable someone such as myself to define a probabilistic model by simply implementing a sampling process (such as the one in the left column of the table in Appendix-2 that defined the model) in a programming language, and then request inference in that model with simple standardized function calls. The implementation of these function calls involves relatively sophisticated software techniques (e.g. tracing and runtime program transformations in order to run backward over the computations that defined the model) but the output is simple: samples from {{latex("P(\\mu, \\sigma|X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(regional)}}, Y=\\mathrm{NIR_{(national)}})")}}. PlanZero uses the combination of Jax and NumPyro as a probabilistic programming system, and the NUTS MCMC algorithm in NumPyro for sampling from the posterior.

Inference in the Static Normals Model

Running NumPyro's NUTS inference algorithm for the static model above, using the NIR-2025 emissions data regarding {{CO2|safe}} emissions from the Harvested Wood Products sector (HWP), results in the following ranges of values for {{latex("\\mu_{\\mathrm{(HWP, CO_2)}}")}} (shown as darker shaded region) and {{latex("\\sigma_{\\mathrm{(HWP, CO_2)}}")}} (shown via lightly-shaded 95% credible intervals on the regional and per-year random variables within {{latex("X_{\\mathrm{(HWP, CO_2)}}")}} and {{latex("Y_{\\mathrm{(HWP, CO_2)}}")}}).

Looking at the modelling results ({{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}}) we see some good and some bad. It is good, that {{latex("\\mu")}} (mean is dotted horizontal line, 95% credible interval is darker shaded region) typically goes through the data points somewhere near the middle. Likewise, {{latex("\\sigma")}} has been estimated such that the data mostly fall within the lightly-shaded regions, with a few points popping out above or below; which is approximately we expect from a 95% {{siteref("Credible Interval", "credible interval")|safe}} after fitting to the data. That said, it is bad that we see large blocks of shaded region without any data, which is a symptom of temporal correlation in the data. (We'll compare this quality of fit with a different model in the next sections.)

Note also that there are differences between the horizontal solid lines (each region's sample mean) and the horizontal dotted lines (each region {{latex("r")}}'s mean {{latex("\\mu_{\\mathrm{(HWP,CO_2,r)}}")}}). The darker shaded region helps put their difference into perspective. The darker shaded region is the 95% credible interval for {{latex("\\mu_{\\mathrm{(HWP,CO_2,r)})}")}} (the upper and lower limits of the central 95% of sampled values from the latent variable's posterior distribution). For two regions, British Columbia and Quebec, the data mean lies slightly outside of their regional {{latex("\\mu_{\\mathrm{(HWP,CO_2,r)}}")}} credibility interval. This means that it is improbable, based on the model, and the data, that either {{latex("\\mu_{\\mathrm{(HWP,CO_2,BC)}}")}} or {{latex("\\mu_{\\mathrm{(HWP,CO_2,QC)}}")}} is really the data mean for each of those provinces. I chose Harvested Wood Products' {{CO2|safe}} emissions because there were a number of years where the regional and national totals are thousands of kt{{CO2|safe}} apart. Unsurprisingly, inference in this model reveals that a discrepancy also exists terms of sample means-since-1990. Remember, the regional latent random variables {{latex("\\mu_{s,g,r}")}} in the model were required to add up to a national total, whereas actual regional and national totals in NIR-2025 do not always add up this way. The inference algorithm has estimated a sample of values for {{latex("\\mu_{s,g,r}")}} that balances (a) fitting the regional emissions data, (b) fitting the national total emissions, and (c) respecting the prior distributions in the model.

This figure doesn't show {{latex("\\mu")|safe}} specifically because it's a standard style of figure for any of probabilistic model of that sector. But it does show the posterior samples of {{latex("\\mu")|safe}} {{latex("\\sigma")|safe}} relate to the set of plausible emissions values.

That concludes the introduction of PlanZero's "Static Normals" model. It is a probabilistic model, but the modelling assumption that each NIR emission sector total is actually unchanging throughout history and into the future is a very untrue assumption for many sectors. There has been consistent significant reduction in emissions from e.g. public electricity production over the past two decades as coal generators have been phased out. On the other hand, the emissions from oil and gas extraction have risen enormously over that same period due to changes in the scale and methods of oil extraction operations. The Static Normals model is designed for simplicity, and it cannot model such effects. The next section introduces a different model based on auto-regressive random processes, which has the property that inference can be described as learning trends in data (this is why machine learning in the field of computer science has that name). The predictions of the AR2 model are more aligned with at least my own, if not most people's intuitions, for how emissions rates change over time.

National Totals vs. Regional Subtotals

Surprisingly, at least to me, regional total emissions in NIR-2025 do not always sum up to the corresponding national total. If we define the sum of regions vs. national total discrepancy as the maximum difference over years and provinces, there are 11/486 with a discrepancy over 1Mt{{CO2e|safe}} (there are 486 sector and GHG combinations). At the level of 100kt{{CO2e}} discrepancy, there are about twice as many combinations over the threshold, and at the level of 10kt{{CO2e}} discrepancy, twice as many again. I defined "Relative Discrepancy" as the discrepancy divided by the greater of itself and the root mean square of national totals, as a way to relate discrepancies to the scale of numbers in their sectors.

For several modest-to-minor sectors, the size of the discrepancy is sometimes at the same order of magnitude as the national total, and even for large sectors such as harvested wood products and [static combustion in] commercial and institutional environments, there are numerically significant discrepancies.

As a consequence for sectors with discrepancies, there is greater uncertainty in the posterior distribution after inference. For sector-gas combinations with discrepancies, the Bayesian inference procedure will rove more widely over possible values for {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}} as there are multiple ways to sort-of-satisfy the objective of making the regional totals likely and sort-of-satisfy the objective of making the national total likely, but no single best way to make all of them likely when the totals simply don't add up.

As an example, let's look at the results of inference for Harvested Wood Products' (HWP) {{CO2|safe}} emissions. {% include "blog/2026-06-28-static-normals-Static_Normals_2024_12_31-HWP.html" %} The National Total (figure above, top left panel) shows the black lines covering more of the upper part of the shaded region than the lower part. The shaded region is the model's prediction of what the emissions might be. The panel shows that the model estimates true emissions as more negative than the reported national data. At the same time, looking at British Columbia, it's the opposite: the model estimates true emissions as less negative than the reported provincial data. The reason that each panel isn't balanced is the reported national total emissions are not the sum of regional reported emissions. The modelled national total is constrained to be the sum of regional totals; the consequence, visible in the figure above, is larger uncertainty in the inference of what's going on with HWP {{CO2}} emissions.

Supporting Update to PlanZero's Build System

The Static Normals model is the first in PlanZero to create build artifacts of a significant size. The samples from the MCMC chains are not huge, but they are tens or hundreds of megabytes, depending on how long they're configured to be. PlanZero's build and deployment implementation was modified not to save these MCMC chains in git, or upload them to fly.io.

PlanZero's Dockerfile was modified to use a multi-stage build to produce a small docker image for fly.io that does not contain large data files or raw model samples.
