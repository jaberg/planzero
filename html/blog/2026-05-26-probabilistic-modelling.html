Introduction

If I were to predict that the Canadian national emissions total for 2025 would be exactly 690.395 Mt{{CO2e|safe}}, it would be silly because (a) how could the total be that exact, specific number, and (b) who would care about that degree of precision when there are a few percentage points worth of error on those totals anyway?

If I were to claim instead that they might be exactly that amount, then again it would be a silly statement because of course it's possible; why not?

The way people have learned to make predictions about such things is to use the language of probability. A {{siteref("Probability Distribution", "probability distribution")|safe}} is a table (or function) of the relative probabilities of all the possible values of an unknown quantity (a {{siteref("Random Variable", "random variable")|safe}}), such the 2025 emissions total in the future 2027 National Inventory Report. The emissions total has to be some value so the probabilities of all of it's possible values add up to 1. A non-silly prediction about the 2025 emissions in the 2027 report might be that "there's a 15% chance the total will be between 690 and 700 Mt{{CO2e|safe}}." That prediction would be the result of adding up the probabilities of values from 690 to 700 according to a probability distribution.

PlanZero has not, until this post, produced probability distributions for e.g. future emission quantities. The post introduces probabilistic modelling via two models: a trivial baseline model that ignores any temporal trends in the data whatsoever (the static model), and non-trivial baseline model based on a two-step autoregressive process that can match trends (the AR-2 model). The static model is simpler, and serves to explain how probabilistic modelling works. Its random variables are easier to visualize. The AR2 model is more complex, but provides a better fit to the data.

Probabilistic Modelling of National Emissions with Static Normals

My mental model of the 2025 National Inventory Report (NIR-2025, or just NIR in this post), is that for each

  • year, from 1990 to 2023,
  • province and territory (I'll call these "regions"),
  • IPCC sector (I'll call these just "sectors"),
  • and greenhouse gas,
there is a quantity, in units of e.g. kt{{CO2e}} that has been estimated in support of Canada's reporting obligations under the Paris Agreement. Let's call this "hypothesis 1". Furtheremore, I believe ("hypothesis 2") that the national totals in the NIR are sums of subtotals from provinces and territories (regions).

Alas, neither of these hypotheses is strictly true. They are true for the majority of years, territories, sectors, and gases, but there are counter-examples to both. Counter to hypothesis 1, there are missing records in the data, such as 81 relating to stationary combustion in the construction industry. Counter to hypothesis 2, regional totals do not always sum up to the national one. At the level of 100kt{{CO2e}} discrepancy, there are about twice as many records, and at the level of 10kt{{CO2e}} discrepancy, twice as many again. {{siteref("Inference Algorithm", "Inference")|safe}} in probabilistic models is about finding values for unobserved random variables that balance two competing objectives: to find values that are probable according to the prior beliefs which are also consisent with observation. The data in the NIR-2025 is not meant to be interpreted too literally. In fact, NIR-2025 includes per-sector estimates of uncertainty on national totals for the base year (1990) and the most-recent year (2023). These uncertainties should be considered a lower bound on our uncertainty about the true sectoral emissions, because for example it can also happen, from time to time, that the protocols for estimating emissions are updated. This happened a few years ago in the estimation of vented {{CH4}}, which caused a re-assessment of the past estimates, and a change that went beyond the previously-estimated uncertainty.

So if we're missing data, and the data we have in the NIR doesn't add up, and anyway the data is published with lower-bounded uncertainty, then what can we say about what really happened? To answer this question, we have to predict what probably happened, and we do this by building a probabilistic model. Such a model consists of

  • random variables for what we observe (e.g. data in the NIR),
  • random variables for what we think should be there (e.g. neat, coherent emissions totals that all add up etc.),
  • mathematical relationships between them (e.g. NIR values might be Normally-distributed around the neat, coherent values).
Within the context of a given model, we can infer the likely values of the neat, coherent variables given what we observe in the NIR data, automatically, using rules of probability theory. (Whether the model is actually good, or which of two models is better, is an important question that I'll come back to in a future post, but leave un-addressed for now.) The next section defines what I'm calling the static model, beginning with a brief refresher of probability notation.

Modelling Harvested Wood Products with Static Normals

One greatly-simplifying modelling hypothesis we could make is that sectoral emissions (per territory, per GHG) aren't actually changing at all over time. With this assumption we can go through the steps of modelling and analysis for a model that is relatively easy to illustrate, compute, etc. Later we will compare it with a model that recognizes temporal trends. This section expresses a probabilistic model of emissions.

Some background on probability notation is required, in order to express probability models.

  • {{latex("P(X=0)")}} means there's a number we don't know, we're calling it {{latex("X")}}, and this is the probability it is actually 0. {{latex("X")}} is called a random variable.
  • {{latex("P(X)")}} is the table (possibly infinitely large, in which case it might be called a function) of all the values {{latex("X")}} might take, and their probabilities (or densities, if the set of values {{latex("X")}} might take is continuous)
  • {{latex("P(0)")}} is sloppy shorthand for {{latex("P(X=0)")}} when it's clear from surrounding context we're talking about {{latex("X")}}
  • {{latex("\\mathcal{N}(\\mu, \\sigma^2)")}} is a Normal distribution, which is non-negative function that sums (integrates) to 1. It is parameterized by two numbers: {{latex("\\mu")}} the mean, and {{latex("\\sigma^2")}} the variance. (Sometimes these are called Gaussians but I'm going to call them Normals to be consistent with the probabilistic programming libraries I'm aware of.)
  • {{latex("X ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(0, 1)")}} means {{latex("X")}} is a random variable, and its corresponding distribution {{latex("P(X)")}} is the thing to the right of the "{{latex("\\sim")}}" symbol, which is a mapping from values to probabilities (or densities if the values are continuous instead of discrete).
  • {{latex("P(A=0, B=1)")}} means there's multiple numbers we don't know, and this is the probability that they both actually have these specific values.
  • {{latex("P(A, B)=P(A)P(B)")}} means that the probability distributions for {{latex("A")|safe}} and {{latex("B")|safe}} are independent. A distribution over multiple random variables is called a joint distribution whether they are independent or not.
  • {{latex("P(A=0| B=1)")}} is the probability that {{latex("A=0")}} if we make an additional assumption about {{latex("B")}}, namely that it is 1. If A and B are not independent, then knowing something about B tells us something about A; in other words, changes what would otherwise have been our {{latex("P(A)")}}.
Probabilistic modelling is a huge topic. I think it's typically introduced in secondary school, and covered in greater detail at various university levels, often with application to areas of science, engineering, medicine, social studies, mathematics etc. where probabilities are used. For more information and orientation, see e.g. wikipedia ( Statistical Model, Graphical Models), many tutorials online specific to probabilistic programming software packages (e.g. Stan, PyMC, Pyro; PlanZero is currently using NumPyro but that might change in the future) or a textbook such as Probabilistic Graphical Models (Koller and Friedman, 2009) for greater depth.

With the two-paragraph crash course in probability out of the way, the following table expresses the static (non-time-varying) probabilistic model model that will be our subject for the rest of this section:
{{latex("\\mu_{(s,g,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(0, 1)")}} Suppose the regional mean emissions are probably around 0, plus or minus 1. It is customary to scale data to be in this range before modelling them, so each emissions time series is scaled to have a mean squared value of 1. After this scaling, plus-or-minus 1 is a relatively un-opinionated prior as to what {{latex("\\mu")}} should be, relative to the NIR data, which will probably say that {{latex("\\mu")}} should be somewhere in the range of plus-or-minus 1.
{{latex("\\sigma_{(s,g,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{LogNormal}(-1, 0.7)")}} Suppose regional variances about the mean are never super-tiny, nor much larger than 1.
{{latex("\\sigma_{(s,g)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{LogNormal}(-1, 0.7)")}} Suppose national variance about the mean are never super-tiny, nor much larger than 1.
{{latex("X_{(s,g,r,t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\mu_{(s,g, r)}, \\sigma_{(s,g, r)}^2)")}} Suppose that for each sector {{latex("s")}}, greenhouse gas {{latex("g")}}, region {{latex("r")}} (province or territory), and time {{latex("t")}} (annual total), suppose there's a number we don't know (random variable X) that's normally distributed about some regional mean {{latex("\\mu_{(s, g, r)}")}}, and with some variance {{latex("\\sigma^2_{(s, g, r, t)}")}}.
{{latex("Y_{(s,g,t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\sum_{r \\in \\mathrm{regions}}\\mu_{(s,g, r)}, \\sigma_{(s,g)}^2)")}} Suppose further that for each sector {{latex("s")}}, greenhouse gas {{latex("g")}}, and time {{latex("t")}} (annual total), some number we don't know (random variable Y) is normally distributed about a mean that is the sum of the regional means, with a variance {{latex("\\sigma^2_{(s, g)}")}}.
The prior densities for the {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}} terms are illustrated below. {{blog.figure_normal()|safe}}

That's it, that's a model of an NIR! The next section is about how we can use an inference algorithm and NIR-2025 data to predict likely neat and coherent latent values ({{latex("\\mu")}}), albeit in the context of this static model.

Inference Algorithms and Probabilistic Programming

The prior model is not really meant to be very accurate, even considering its structural limitation of being a static model. To fix it up, the expectation is to perform some amount of inference. The natural inference for this model is to treat the actual NIR as observations of our {{latex("X")|safe}} and {{latex("Y")|safe}} random variables and infer what {{latex("\\mu")|safe}} and {{latex("\\sigma")|safe}} probably were, given both our prior about what they would be, and the observation of X and Y (which depend on {{latex("\\mu")|safe}} and {{latex("\\sigma")|safe}}). Bayesian inference addresses the question of what {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}} probably were in terms of a joint probabilility distribution:

{{latex("P(\\mu, \\sigma|X=\\mathrm{NIR^{(2025, regional)}}, Y=\\mathrm{NIR^{(2025, national)}})", "block")}}

This [joint] distribution is specifically a posterior joint distribution because it's conditioned on data. Latent [random] variables are the variables in our model that are not observed, i.e. for which we don't have data. Generally, the posterior distribution over a model's latent variables is not a distribution with a neat and tidy formula such as a normal or exponential. Typically the only practical way to work with these distributions is with algorithms, such as the inference algorithms mentioned in the previous section. Inference algorithms work on the basis of Bayes' Theorem. In standard probability notation, Bayes' theorem looks like:

{{latex("P(A|B) = \\frac{P(A, B)}{P(B)} = \\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}", "block")}}.

Bayes' theorem shows how to relate distributions of the form {{latex("P(A|B)")}}, to ones involving {{latex("P(B|A)")}} instead. With the help of Bayes theorem, it is possible to transform a posterior distribution we're interested in, such as {{latex("P(\\mu, \\sigma|X=\\mathrm{NIR^{(2025, regional)}},~Y = \\mathrm{NIR^{(2025, national)}})")}}, into a formula with the sorts of pieces with which we specified our model (just looking at the numerator, the terms here don't look exactly like the ones in the table above, but can you spot the correspondence?):

{{latex( "\\frac{P(X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, regional)}},~Y = \\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, national)}}|\\mu, \\sigma)P(\\mu)P(\\sigma)} {P(X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, regional)}},~Y = \\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, national)}})}", "block")}}.

Probabilistic programming systems enable someone such as myself to write down a generative sampling processes (such as the one in the left column of the table above) in a programming language, and leverage standard algorithm implementations to inference. Even with a Ph.D. in machine learning, I still find these algorithms amazing (read up on e.g. MCMC algorithms or stochastic variational inference if you're interested in how these algorithms work). The implementation of these algorithms can involve relatively sophisticated software techniques (e.g. tracing and runtime program transformations) and the algorithms themselves benefit from ongoing statistics research. For our purposes we'll take their capabilities as given (specifically via the use of Jax and NumPyro, and the NUTS MCMC algorithm), and apply them, in the next section, to draw samples from the posterior distribution {{latex("P(\\mu, \\sigma|X=\\mathrm{NIR^{(2025, regional)}}, Y=\\mathrm{NIR^{(2025, national)}})")}} assuming the static model described above.

On the good side, the estimated mean {{latex("\\mu")}} (solid horizontal line) looks to be fit reasonably, in that it typically goes through the data points somewhere near the middle. This means that it is hard to believe, based on the model, and the data, that {{latex("\\mu_{s,g,r}")}} is exactly the data mean for each of those provinces. I chose Harvested Wood Products' {{CO2|safe}} emissions because there were a number of years where the regional and national totals are thousands of kt{{CO2|safe}} apart. Perhaps unsurprisingly, inference in this model reveals that a discrepancy also exists terms of means-since-1990. The regional latent {{latex("\\mu_{s,g,r}")}} values in the model are required to add up to the national total, whereas regional totals in NIR-2025 are not. The inference algorithm has estimated a sample of values for {{latex("\\mu_{s,g,r}")}} that balances (a) fitting the regional emissions data, (b) fitting the national total emissions, and (c) respecting the prior distributions in the model.

That concludes PlanZero's first probabilistic model of a sector and gas (Harvested Wood Products {{CO2|safe}}). To say whether it is a good model requires an alternative and a basis for comparison. The next two sections introduce a second probabilistic model and an evaluation metric for comparing them.

An Alternative Model based on an Autoregressive Process

It is natural to view the emissions data in the NIR as time series. A model should leverage the fact that emissions tend to change slowly; the emissions from the year before are usually a good guess for the current year, and probably a better guess than the overall mean since 1990. There are many approaches and applications of time-series analysis but this section develops an emissions model based on autoregressive analysis. An auto-regressive process of order {{latex("p")|safe}}, called an AR-{{latex("p")|safe}} process, is a random walk that can be written like this:

{{latex( " \\begin{array} Z_{1..p} & \\sim & \\mathcal{N}(0, \\sigma^2) \\\\ Z_t & \\sim & \\mathcal{N}(c_0 + c_1 Z_{t-1} + c_2 Z_{t-2} + ... + c_p Z_{t-p}, \\sigma^2) \\end{array} ", "block").replace("", '') }}

Such a random variable {{latex("Z")|safe}} is said to be generated by an AR-{{latex("p")|safe}} process. An autoregressive process proceeds by adding some combination of its previous elements together with a constant offset, and then adding some randomness according to {{latex("\\sigma^2")|safe}}. The initial {{latex("p")|safe}} steps of the process need a different definition; above they are defined to be independent variables.

The following time-varying graphical model for NIR data will be our subject for the rest of this section. (Indexes by {{latex("s,g")|safe}} are dropped here for brevity, and because anyway all sectors and gases are considered to be independent. So for example {{latex("\\mu_{(s,g, r, t)}")|safe}} is written {{latex("\\mu_{(r, t)}")|safe}}.)
{{latex("\\forall_{r}\\forall_{t\\in\\{1,2\\}} ~ \\alpha_{r,t} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{Kumaraswamy}(1.25, 1.25)")}} Draw some numbers between 0 and 1, not too close to those limits, two for each region.
{{latex("c_{r,1} ~=~ .1 + 1.5\\alpha_{r,1}")}} Let the coefficient on the previous timestep range between .1 and 1.6.
{{latex("c_{r,2} ~=~ -.5 + \\alpha_{r,2}")}} Let the coefficient on the 2nd-previous timestep range between -0.5 and 0.5.
{{latex("\\forall_{r}\\forall_{t\\in\\{1,2\\}} ~ \\mu_{r,t} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(0, 1)")}} Let the first two steps of the autoregressive process for each region be independent normals.
{{latex("\\sigma_{r}=\\max(\\sqrt{\\frac{1}{T}\\sum_t^T e_{r,t}^2}, 1)")}} Even after scaling the whole emissions data set so that the national totals had a dynamic range of about 1, peek at the provincial and territorial emissions data to define a scale for each of these regions ("e" is for the regional emissions after scaling by the national factor.)
{{latex("\\mu_{r,t} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(c_{r,1} \\mu_{r,t-1} + c_{r,2} \\mu_{r,t-2}, 0.05^2 \\sigma_{r}^2)")}} Suppose a latent {{latex("\\mu_{(r,t)}")|safe}} variable varies with time according to an AR-2 process. The {{latex(".05^2 \\sigma_r^2")|safe}} term here controls how quickly the AR-2 process can change course from where its previous values and weights {{latex("c")|safe}} would otherwise take it. Inference is quite sensitive to this parameter.
{{latex("X_{r,t} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\mu_{(r, t)}, (0.05\\sigma_{r, t})^2)")}} Similar to the static model, except that {{latex("\\mu_{(r, t)}")|safe}} is indexed by year {{latex("t")|safe}}, so that our model is time-varying. Variance in this model was fixed, based roughly on the uncertainty estimate in the NIR-2025, which was 19% for 1990 and 4% in 2023.
{{latex("Y_{t} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\sum_{r \\in \\mathrm{regions}}\\mu_{r,t}, 0.05^2)")}} Similar to the static model, except that we use a time-indexed {{latex("\\mu_{r, t}")|safe}} here too. Variance in this model was fixed, based roughly on the uncertainty estimate for NIR-2025, which was 19% for 1990 and 4% in 2023.

Comparing Models as Predictors of the Future

For every purpose to which a model might be put, it is possible to ask how well-suited it is to that purpose, and perhaps, which of two particular models is better-suited. One way to decide which model is better is to assign each a score and then compare the scores. In machine learning, evaluation metrics are numerical scores for models, as applied to a particular modelling task. To the extent that the fitness of a model for some purpose can be estimated accurately with an evaluation metric, the job of finding a the best models for that purpose becomes an optimization problem. Framing an optimization problem can be helpful because both people and computers are good at solving optimization problems; optimization problem statements clarify what is important and what can be ignored, at least within the context of that optimization. The machine learning community has made great progress over recent decades by focusing model development around standard problems. I hope that this practice may serve to support emission-reduction efforts as well, so long as standard, relevant problems can be identified.

As a first standard challenge, this post introduces a prediction challenge which if not necessarily of long-term importance, can at least serve to show how the models above can be compared numerically. The challenge is slightly tricky to describe, so I've provided the following diagram to support the explanation below. The "Year-Out, Last-Year (YOLY-2025)" prediction challenge is to predict, on the basis of data that was available one year ahead of its publication, the most-recent year's emissions that were covered of the NIR-2025 National Inventory Report, which was year 2023. (Technically, I should use the NIR-2024 data for this evaluation, but I'm cheating and using years 1990-2022 from NIR-2025 instead because it's the data I have at hand.)

The NIR-2025 Year-Out, Last-Year (YOLY-2025) prediction challenge (of year 2023): {{blog.figure_YOLY_2025()|safe}}

Both of our models (I'll call them "Const" and "AR2") can predict 2023 emissions in NIR-2025 based on previous years' data. When I ran inference on these models to make the figures above, I generated a sample of 1000 possible assignments of the latent variables, which were (ideally) distributed according to each model's posterior distribution conditioned on data from years 1990-2022. Every single one of these latent variable assignments can make a prediction for year 2023. The distributions of these predictions for Canada and each province, and territory, for {{CO2|safe}} emissions from Harvested Wood Products, is shown in the figure below.

{{blog.figure_YOLY_2025_violinplots()|safe}}

Both models make good predictions, in that the range of values they predict includes the actual 2023 emissions data (the solid dot on each violin plot). However, the models are different in that the range of values predicted by the Const model for each category is relatively large (e.g. 140 Mt{{CO2e|safe}}) in the case of the national total), and by comparison the range of predictions from the AR2 model for each category is smaller (e.g. only about 25 Mt{{CO2e|safe}} for the national total.) Qualitatively, we can see that the AR2 model is making much bolder predictions, and the data for this sector reveal that it also made reasonably correct predictions. The AR2 model is the better predictor, as far as our data is concerned.

A standard way to to turn this qualitative assessment into a model evaluation metric is compute the predicted probability of the evaluation data, according to the model. In the case of this data, I think the job is to predict all of the provincial and territorial totals and the national total together, with one bad prediction ruining the whole lot. The YOLY-2025 evaluation metric {{latex("\\mathcal{L}")}} is the entire year's emission data, as estimated by the model.

{{latex(" \\mathcal{L} = \\log\\left( P(X_{(t=2023)}=\\mathrm{NIR}^{(2023)}) \\right) ", "block")}}

To use this formula on our models we must transform it into an expectation over latent variables ({{latex("\\theta")}}) so that we can evaluate it with the sample ({{latex("\\hat \\theta")}}) that we computed by inference of the posterior distribution.

{{latex(" \\mathcal{L} = \\log\\left( \\int_\\theta P(X_{(t=2023)}=\\mathrm{NIR}^{(2023)}|\\theta) P(\\theta) \\right) \\approx \\log\\left( \\frac{1}{n} \\sum_{\\hat \\theta_i ~\\sim~ P(\\theta)}^n P(X_{(t=2023)}=\\mathrm{NIR}^{(2023)}|\\hat \\theta_i) \\right) ", "block")}}

This expression can be computed for each model, at least for {{CO2|safe}} from Harvested Wood Products. The results are in the table below.

ModelEvaluation Data Log-Probability {{latex("\\mathcal{L}")}} on {{CO2|safe}} from Harvested Wood Products
Const (non-time-varying){{"{:.3f}".format(blog.log_prob_2023_const())}}
AR2-based model{{"{:.3f}".format(blog.log_prob_2023_AR2())}}

The log-probability of the 2023 data under both models is negative. Each emission data point is modelled as Normal with a scale spanning up to tens of Mt{{CO2e|safe}}, so most of them are stretched pretty thinly (distributions always have to sum or integrate to 1.0). The logarithm of a tiny positive number is a large negative number. The logarithm of a product of tiny positive numbers (such as the probabilities of the data points for each province and territory) is the sum of the logarithm of each one, and with each logarithm being negative, the sum is an even larger negative number. The log probability of our evaluation data under the constant model is about -107. The log probability of our evaluation data under the AR-2 model is about -103. It may seem that -107 is close to -103 because they're separated by 4%, but technically the results are that the actual 2023 emissions data are about {{latex("e^{-103.2-(-107)}\\approx50")}} times more probable under the AR2 model than the Constant one. The AR2 model predicted the 2023 emissions more precisely, and it turned out to be right.

Log-probability isn't a perfect evaluation metric, but it's pretty good. As one example of a shortcoming, it can be gamed on Harvested Wood Products by a model that makes infinite confidence prediction that Nunavut's HWP emissions will be exactly 0. That particular silliness can be avoided in a couple of ways (e.g. make sure all models handle easy predictions similarly, or add noise to data as part of the evaluation metric), but in general, it's important to use all the tools, cleverness, and data available to us to recognize and prevent such silliness for ruining an evaluation metric that's otherwise quite useful. Log probability isn't a perfect evaluation metric, but I believe with a little vigilence, it's a good one; by iteratively incorporating models with superior evaluation log-likelihood on YOLY-202X prediction challenges, PlanZero models can get quite good at year-out last-year NIR prediction.

Conclusion

  • Introduced hierarchical Bayesian modelling
  • Introduced two models of a challenging sector, which features significant disagreement between provincial and national estimates.
  • Illustrated them qualitiatively
  • Introduced an evaluation metric reflective of 1-year-out prediction of forthcoming NIR publication, for year 2023 specifically.
  • Compared the two models quantitatively as predictors of 2023 emissions; the AR2 model was quantitatively superior, reflecting what I think was relatively obvious qualitative judgement.

Probabilistic modelling is compatible with the scenario analysis used to evaluate strategies (like e.g. Bovaer adoption), and for strategies that are already active, inference algorithms can even assess whether strategies are working as intended (at least relative to data and a model); but illustrating how this can be done will wait for a future post.

It's interesting to consider a hypothetical YOLY-2027 challenge, because the "prediction time" for that challenge has passed (April 2026) but the NIR-2027 has not yet been published. Despite not yet being able to score models on YOLY-2027, YOLY-2027 is perhaps the most interesting of the three because, it is possible to make the models, and their predictions might have value precisely because the actual NIR-2027 publication is not available yet. (Strictly speaking, the YOLY-2025 challenge should use the previously-published NIR-2024 to get data up to year 2022 for model development and posterior inference / training. I'm using earlier years from NIR-2025 as a short-cut. This short-cut isn't possible for NIR-2027 because indeed, only NIR-2026 has been published at this time.)

On the topic of modelling harvested wood products specifically (among NIR-sectors) this post represents a step back for the sake of two future steps forward. The previous PlanZero post on Harvested Wood products summarized research on how HWP emissions are calculated in terms of historical volumes of logs and bolts, wood for pulp and paper and so on. This post ignores all of that and models HWP emissions as a random walk. The reconciliation of these two types of model is left to future work. The broad outline of that future work is clear: model instead the volumes of e.g. harvested logs and bolts as the random walk, and use the appropriate simulation-style mathematical expressions to convert hypothesized volumes of bolts to HWP emissions in subsequent years. The likely challenge lurking within the broad outline of work is that inference in such a model may not be effective in the first paramaterization I try. Furthermore, I suspect this will not be a one-off challenge.

I believe that probabilistic modelling represents a new standard for what it means to add an NIR sector model to PlanZero. I expect the work for many future posts will be to find reasonable and useful priors and model structures that reflect contributing factors to the various NIR sectors. I will focus first on incorporating NIR sectors that I've written about in PlanZero already.
