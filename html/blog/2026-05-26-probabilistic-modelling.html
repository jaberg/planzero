Introduction

PlanZero has used many kinds of data to date, starting with the 2025 {{siteref("NIR")}}, it has visualized that data, and it has simulated a strategy on the basis of that data and some additional assumptions, but it has not employed probabilistic modelling. Probabilistic modelling is the construction of probabilistic models (relationships between random variables) and inference of how those variables might correspond to actual data (observations). Probabilistic modelling is good for prediction with quantified uncertainty: prediction of the future, prediction of past statistics that were not directly observed, or even prediction of past statistics that were directly observed but with insufficient precision. This post introduces probabilistic modelling to PlanZero.

The post introduces probabilistic modelling via two models: a straw-man "Constant" model, and a demonstrably and qualitatively superior 2-step autoregressive "AR2" model. The Constant model is simpler, and serves to explain how probabilistic modelling works because its random variables are easier to visualize. The AR2 model is more complex, but more realistic (it reflects that the rates of emission in a sector can change over time, which the Constant model ignores). With two models at hand, the post explains how models can be compared quantitatively for a particular task of future-emissions-prediction.

Before we get into the models and model comparisons though, the first section introduces the technique of probabilistic modelling by motivating why PlanZero would employ it, when after all, probabilistic modelling is more mathematically and computationally demanding than the the simulation-based modelling employed so far.

Prediction with Quantified Confidence: the Motivation for Probabilic Modelling

PlanZero's mission calls for both short-, medium-, and long-term prediction capabilities suitable for the evaluation of strategies. The credibility and usefulness of model predictions rests on three pillars:

  1. the structure of the model stands to reason
  2. the predictions refine people's prior beliefs, rather than contradicting them
  3. the predictions agree with data, where data is available, while also being precise

Simulation based-modelling is not generally strong across all of these areas. What I mean by simulation-based modelling is what might be described as spreadsheet-style modelling; from a computational perspective, the modelling proceeds from data and initial numerical assumptions, through arithmetic and statistical summaries, forward in time, and with one pass through the modelled time period of the model, it arrives at a result. This is the kind of modelling that PlanZero has employed to date. Simulation-based modelling is good with regards to criterion (1) in that predictions can be reasonably explained directly in terms of the computation of the model. It is not great with regards to criterion (2) because simulation-based modelling does not naturally express how [un]likely a given possible future is, it just emits its own prediction. Critically though, simulation-based modelling offers very little support for matching data. Data matching is entirely at the discretion of the modeller, and the result of simulation does not inherently characterize whether data was either used or fit well.

Probabilistic modelling is a generalization of simulation-based modelling that can produce more-credible models. In probabilistic modelling (particularly so-called directed graphical modelling) modelling starts with random variables instead of numbers. So for example, instead of starting with e.g. the NIR data, a probabilistic model starts with the idea that there are emission amounts in such-and-such sectors of such-and-such gases (random variables), and that there are these emissions for all of the years that we care to include in our model, we just don't know, a-priori exactly how much emissions there were in each year. When I say we don't know exactly how much emissions there were, I mean that probabilistic modelling encourages the expression of some idea how much emissions there were, even if it's a broad range. That initial belief has a name in probabilistic modelling, it's called a prior. We might have a prior that {{CO2}} emissions from national electricity generation are relatively big, but still smaller than e.g. global emissions. As a contrasting example, we might express that the {{NF3}} emissions from a sector that isn't known for producing that gas, in a province that isn't known for activity in that sector, is probably zero, at least for most years.

The relationship between this probabilistic modelling and simulation-based modelling is if we combine the random variables with arithmetic and statistical expressions (like we would do in a simulation-based model), then drawing a sample from all the random variables at once looks a lot like running a simulation-based model. This computation has a name in probabilistic modelling: sampling from the prior. It's kind of fun, to see your prior beliefs transformed into sets of numbers that look like hypothetical NIRs because they are our own predictions of the future! Looking back at our list of criteria for making credible predictions, a probabilistic model is similar to a simulation-based one in terms of model structure (criterion 1), and it's better than a single scenario from a simulation-based model at refining rather than contradicting beliefs (criterion 2) because it's natural to draw lots of scenarios from a probabilistic model, and the spread of scenarios will include people's beliefs and communicate some degree of [dis]agreement. Where probabilistic models really excel though, is with regards to agreeing with data (criterion 3), which I'll explain in the next section.

Inference (aka tuning, fitting, training)

Where probabilistic modelling really go above and beyond simulation-based modelling, is when it's possible to use some data to refine a model's priors before making predictions. Using data in this way can make a night vs. day difference to the quality of prediction. To illustrate by way of the running example of {{CO2|safe}} from national electricity generation, I mean something almost too obvious to state: if we want our model to make predictions for e.g. year 2023 that agree with the data (while also being precise) it is more helpful to incorporate reported historical emissions from e.g. years 1990 to 2022 (not to mention other data sources that might be even more informative), than to ignore that data and guess directly from our prior beliefs. Inference algorithms operate on a model to incorporate data automatically in ways that respect rules of probability theory. The result of inference is a posterior distribution over the random variables in a model, which typically refines them from broad vague distributions, to more precise ones. In the case that we had modelled emissions as coming from a non-time-varying normal distribution with a large a-priori variance, inference would refine that prior to be something like the empirical mean and variance across years 1990 to 2022.

Probabilistic modelling, particularly paired with appropriate data sources and the use of inference algorithms, is admittedly a broad family of approaches, but it encompasses the the state of the art in accurate prediction. The techniques for assessing and optimizing the fit of probalisitic models to data are well established. When it comes to making predictions that agree with data, probabilistic modelling with inference is the way to go; simulation-based modelling is not.

A probabilistic model is a hypothesis about how some aspect of the world works, which makes predictions about some data that we have. Taking the NIR-2025 for example, as our data set, we might hypothesize (hypothesis 1) that for each

  • year, from 1990 to 2023,
  • province and territory (I'll call these just "territories"),
  • IPCC sector (I'll call these just "sectors"),
  • and greenhouse gas,
there is a quantity, in units of e.g. kt{{CO2e}} that has been estimated in support of Canada's reporting obligations under the Paris Agreement. Furtheremore, we might hypothesize (hypothesis 2) that the various national totals are sums of these territorial emissions.

Bayesian reasoning involves trading off two sensible but not-always-compatible ways to think: trusting your hypothesis about how you think things work (sometimes called your prior beliefs), and trusting your observations about what is actually happening. The data in the NIR-2025 is not meant to be interpreted too literally. In fact, NIR-2025 includes per-sector estimates of uncertainty on national totals for the base year (1990) and the most-recent year (2023). These uncertainties should be considered a lower boundon our uncertainty about these numbers. It can also happen, from time to time, that the protocols for estimating emissions are updated; this happened a few years ago in the area of vented {{CH4}}, which causes a re-assessment of the past estimates, and a change that went beyond the previously-estimated uncertainty. Maybe there really was a set of territorial estimates that added up the published national totals, it was just that one or more provinces and territories estimated the wrong values. Or maybe the provinces used more appropriate data and the national estimate was off. Or maybe they were both off. A probabilistic model together with a Bayesian inference algorithm can produce posterior estimates of what most-plausibly actually happened given the data, its uncertainty, and a conviction that territorial totals should add up to national ones.

Modelling NIR-2025 with Constants

One greatly-simplifying modelling hypothesis we could make is that sectoral emissions (per territory, per GHG) aren't actually changing at all over time, we just measure them in some way that makes it appear as if they are changing from year to year according to trends (but such trends are just in our imagination if they're anywhere at all). My goal with this section isn't to investigate a model that anyone in particular believes to be true, it's to go through the steps of modelling and analysis for a model that is relatively simple to illustrate, communicate, etc. We will see later, that this is a less-likely model than one that recognizes temporal trends. (Happily, this was also my intuition.)

Some background on probability notation is required, in order to express probability models.

  • {{latex("P(X=0)")}} means there's a number we don't know, we're calling it {{latex("X")}}, and this is the probability it is actually 0. {{latex("X")}} is called a random variable.
  • {{latex("P(X)")}} is the table (possibly infinitely large in theory) of all the values {{latex("X")}} might take, and their probabilities
  • {{latex("P(0)")}} is sloppy shorthand for {{latex("P(X=0)")}} when it's clear from surrounding context we're talking about {{latex("X")}}
  • {{latex("\\mathcal{N}(\\mu, \\sigma^2)")}} is a Normal distribution, which is non-negative function that sums (integrates) to 1. It is parameterized by two numbers: {{latex("\\mu")}} the mean, and {{latex("\\sigma^2")}} the variance.
  • {{latex("X ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(0, 1)")}} means {{latex("X")}} is a random variable, and its corresponding distribution {{latex("P(X)")}} is the thing to the right of the "{{latex("\sim")}}" symbol
  • {{latex("P(A=0, B=1)")}} means there's multiple numbers we don't know, and this is the probability that they both actually have these specific values.
  • {{latex("P(A=0| B=1)")}} is the probability that {{latex("A=0")}} if we make an additional assumption about {{latex("B")}}, namely that it is 1. If A and B are not independent, then knowing something about B tells us something about A; in other words, changes what would otherwise have been our {{latex("P(A)")}}.
Probabilistic modelling is a huge topic. I think it's typically introduced in secondary school, and covered in greater detail at various university levels, often with application to areas of science, engineering, medicine, social studies, mathematics etc. where probabilities are used. For more information and orientation, see e.g. wikipedia ( Statistical Model, Graphical Models), many tutorials online specific to probabilistic programming software packages (e.g. Stan, PyMC, Pyro; PlanZero is currently using NumPyro but that might change in the future) or a textbook such as Probabilistic Graphical Models (Koller and Friedman, 2009) for greater depth.

The following non-time-varying graphical model for NIR data will be our subject for the rest of this section.
{{latex("X_{(s,g,r,t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\mu_{(s,g, r)}, \\sigma_{(s,g, r, t)}^2)")}} Suppose that for each sector {{latex("s")}}, greenhouse gas {{latex("g")}}, region {{latex("r")}} (province or territory), and time {{latex("t")}} (annual total), suppose there's a number we don't know (random variable X) that's normally distributed about some regional mean {{latex("\\mu_{(s, g, r)}")}}, and with some variance {{latex("\\sigma^2_{(s, g, r, t)}")}}.
{{latex("Y_{(s,g,t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\sum_{r \in \\mathrm{regions}}\\mu_{(s,g, r)}, \\sigma_{(s,g)}^2)")}} Suppose further that for each sector {{latex("s")}}, greenhouse gas {{latex("g")}}, and time {{latex("t")}} (annual total), some number we don't know (random variable Y) is normally distributed about a mean that is the sum of the regional means, with a variance {{latex("\\sigma^2_{(s, g)}")}}.
{{latex("\\mu_{(s,g,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(0, 1)")}} Suppose the regional mean emissions are probably around 0, plus or minus 10. It is customary to scale data to be in this range before modelling them, so each emissions time series is scaled to have a mean squared value of 1. After this scaling, plus-or-minus 10 is a relatively un-opinionated prior as to what {{latex("\\mu")}} should be, relative to the NIR data, which will probably say that {{latex("\\mu")}} should be somewhere in the range of plus-or-minus 1.
{{latex("\\sigma_{(s,g,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{LogNormal}(-1, 0.7)")}} Suppose regional variances about the mean are never super-tiny, nor much larger than 1.
{{latex("\\sigma_{(s,g)} ~\\sim~ \mathcal{LogNormal}(-1, 0.7)")}} Suppose national variance about the mean are never super-tiny, nor much larger than 1.
The prior densities for the {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}} terms are illustrated below. {{blog.figure_normal()|safe}}

Having made the suppositions above, and two final ones — namely that {{latex("X = \\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, regional)}}")}} and {{latex("Y = \\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, national)}}")}} — we have ourselves a model of NIR-2025. With it, we can figure out what are {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}}. In the model, they are described as (a) coming from a normal and log-normal prior distributions respectively, but then (b) also being good explanations for the NIR-2025 data. Reconciling these two competing objectives is what makes probabilistic modelling interesting. Below, we'll see how {{latex("\\mu")}}, in particular, compares with the empirical mean of each provincial emission in a sector where the difference is easiest to see.

The Bayesian answer to the question of what are {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}} is a joint probabilility distribution:

{{latex("P(\\mu, \\sigma|X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, regional)}}, Y=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, national)}})", "block")}}

This distribution is a called a posterior distribution because it's conditioned on data, and it's over latent variables, the ones hypothesized within the model that aren't observed directly. Generally, the posterior distribution of a graphical model's latent variables is not a distribution with a neat and tidy formula such as a normal or exponential. Often the only practical way to work with these distributions is with algorithms, such as inference algorithms. The next section uses an inference algorithm called NUTS in the NumPyro software library to draw samples from {{latex("P(\\mu, \\sigma|X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, regional)}}, Y=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, national)}})")}} under our modelling assumptions.

Probabilistic Programming and Inference

Algorithmic inference in graphical models is enabled by Bayes' theorem. In standard probability notation, Bayes' theorem looks like:

{{latex("P(A|B) = \\frac{P(A, B)}{P(B)} = \\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}", "block")}}.

Bayes' theorem shows how to relate distributions of the form {{latex("P(A|B)")}}, to ones involving {{latex("P(B|A)")}} instead. With the help of Bayes theorem, it is possible to transform a posterior distribution we're interested in, such as {{latex("P(\\mu, \\sigma|X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, regional)}},~Y = \\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, national)}})")}}, into a formula with the sorts of pieces with which we specified our model:

{{latex( "\\frac{P(X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, regional)}},~Y = \\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, national)}}|\\mu, \\sigma)P(\\mu)P(\\sigma)} {P(X=\\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, regional)}},~Y = \\mathrm{NIR_{(2025, national)}})}", "block")}}.

An inference algorithm can act on a system of generative probability distributions, combine it with observations of some of the variables, some of the time, and infer what the latent variables were, in the form of e.g. a sample from the posterior distribution. Probabilistic programming systems enable someone such as myself to write down nested generative sampling processes like those in the first column in the table above in a programming language, and leverage standard inference algorithm implementations. Even with a Ph.D. in machine learning, I still find these algorithms amazing (read up on e.g. MCMC algorithms or stochastic variational inference if you're interested in how these things work). The design of general sampling algorithms continues to be an area of research in statistics and machine learning communities, but for our purposes we'll take their capabilities as given, and apply them to the inference of {{latex("\\mu")}} and {{latex("\\sigma")}} given the NIR-2025 data.

Modelling NIR-2025 with an AR2 Process

Candidly, I think that a fixed distribution, one that doesn't change from year to year, is a poor model of many historical emissions (including most of the ones above from the Harvested Wood Products sector). I also think that there's a lot of temporal correlation from one year to the next. One way to test these beliefs is to model them, fit that model to data with an inference algorithm (just like we did in the previous section) and see what kinds of inferences (and predictions) it makes. This section develops an alternative model to the constant model discussed in the previous section. The next section compares the two models as predictors of future emissions.

There are many approaches and applications of time-series analysis but this section develops an emissions model based on Autoregressive Analysis. Bayesian autoregressive analysis results from fitting a model with a latent time series (e.g. {{latex("Z")|safe}}, below) that progresses according to a random walk. An auto-regressive process of order {{latex("p")|safe}}, is called an AR-{{latex("p")|safe}} process, and it can be written like this:

{{latex( "Z_t ~\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(c_0 + c_1 Z_{t-1} + c_2 Z_{t-2} + ... + c_p Z_{t-p}, \sigma^2)", "block")}}

Such a random variable {{latex("Z")|safe}} is said to be generated by an AR-{{latex("p")|safe}} process. An autoregressive process proceeds by adding some combination of its previous elements together with a constant offset, and then adding some randomness according to {{latex("\sigma^2")|safe}}. The initial {{latex("p")|safe}} values have to be specified in some other way.

Comparing Models as Predictors of the Future

For every purpose to which a model might be put, it is possible to ask how well-suited it is to that purpose, and perhaps, which of two particular models is better-suited. One way to decide which model is better is to assign each a score and then compare the scores. In machine learning, evaluation metrics are numerical scores for models, as applied to a particular modelling task. To the extent that the fitness of a model for some purpose can be estimated accurately with an evaluation metric, the job of finding a the best models for that purpose becomes an optimization problem. Framing an optimization problem can be helpful because both people and computers are good at solving optimization problems; optimization problem statements clarify what is important and what can be ignored, at least within the context of that optimization. The machine learning community has made great progress over recent decades by focusing model development around standard problems. I hope that this practice may serve to support emission-reduction efforts as well, so long as standard, relevant problems can be identified.

As a first standard challenge, this post introduces a prediction challenge which if not necessarily of long-term importance, can at least serve to show how the models above can be compared numerically. The challenge is slightly tricky to describe, so I've provided the following diagram to support the explanation below. The "Year-Out, Last-Year (YOLY-2025)" prediction challenge is to predict, on the basis of data that was available one year ahead of its publication, the most-recent year's emissions that were covered of the NIR-2025 National Inventory Report, which was year 2023. (Technically, I should use the NIR-2024 data for this evaluation, but I'm cheating and using years 1990-2022 from NIR-2025 instead because it's the data I have at hand.)

The NIR-2025 Year-Out, Last-Year (YOLY-2025) prediction challenge (of year 2023): {{blog.figure_YOLY_2025()|safe}}

Both of our models (I'll call them "Const" and "AR2") can predict 2023 emissions in NIR-2025 based on previous years' data. Every single one of these latent variable assignments can make a prediction for year 2023. A standard way to to turn this qualitative assessment into a model evaluation metric is compute the predicted probability of the evaluation data, according to the model. In the case of this data, I think the job is to predict all of the provincial and territorial totals and the national total together, with one bad prediction ruining the whole lot. The YOLY-2025 evaluation metric {{latex("\\mathcal{L}")}} is the entire year's emission data, as estimated by the model.

{{latex(" \\mathcal{L} = \\log\\left( P(X_{(t=2023)}=\\mathrm{NIR}^{(2023)}) \\right) ", "block")}}

To use this formula on our models we must transform it into an expectation over latent variables ({{latex("\\theta")}}) so that we can evaluate it with the sample ({{latex("\\hat \\theta")}}) that we computed by inference of the posterior distribution.

{{latex(" \\mathcal{L} = \\log\\left( \int_\\theta P(X_{(t=2023)}=\\mathrm{NIR}^{(2023)}|\\theta) P(\\theta) \\right) \\approx \\log\\left( \\frac{1}{n} \sum_{\\hat \\theta_i ~\\sim~ P(\\theta)}^n P(X_{(t=2023)}=\\mathrm{NIR}^{(2023)}|\\hat \\theta_i) \\right) ", "block")}}

The log-probability of the 2023 data under both models is negative. Each emission data point is modelled as Normal with a scale spanning up to tens of Mt{{CO2e|safe}}, so most of them are stretched pretty thinly (distributions always have to sum or integrate to 1.0). The logarithm of a tiny positive number is a large negative number. The logarithm of a product of tiny positive numbers (such as the probabilities of the data points for each province and territory) is the sum of the logarithm of each one, and with each logarithm being negative, the sum is an even larger negative number. The log probability of our evaluation data under the constant model is about -107. The log probability of our evaluation data under the AR-2 model is about -103. It may seem that -107 is close to -103 because they're separated by 4%, but technically the results are that the actual 2023 emissions data are about {{latex("e^{-103.2-(-107)}\\approx50")}} times more probable under the AR2 model than the Constant one. The AR2 model predicted the 2023 emissions more precisely, and it turned out to be right.

Conclusion

  • Introduced hierarchical Bayesian modelling
  • Introduced two models of a challenging sector, which features significant disagreement between provincial and national estimates.
  • Illustrated them qualitiatively
  • Introduced an evaluation metric reflective of 1-year-out prediction of forthcoming NIR publication, for year 2023 specifically.
  • Compared the two models quantitatively as predictors of 2023 emissions; the AR2 model was quantitatively superior, reflecting what I think was relatively obvious qualitative judgement.
