Introduction

A few months ago, a post called Modelling a Bovaer Strategy showed how predictions of the future might be influenced by a {{ siteref("Strategy", "strategy") }} (the strategy was about administering a feed additive to cattle that reduces emissions from enteric fermentation). The previous PlanZero post, Static Normals: a Baseline Predictive Model, introduced a different way of predicting the future, namely by extending a statistical pattern. This post is about combining both: it introduces a probabilistic "Scaling" model whose predictions are based both on statistical patterns and the influence of a probabilistic interpretation of the "Scale Bovaer" strategy that is similar to the deterministic one introduced previously.

As before, the "Scale Bovaer" strategy implements the idea that some fraction of cattle farmers would be open to starting to administer Bovaer if they were sufficiently incentivized by government subsidy (and that no statistically significant population of farmers reverts to Bovaer-free farming). The model assumes, as before, that a sufficient incentive would be $5000 per year. (All $-denominated values in this site are in CAD unless otherwise indicated.) Differences relative to the previous deterministic model:

  • Whereas the deterministic model fixed the fraction of farmers-open-to-switching-per-year at 5%, the probabilistic model varies it from 2.5-10% uniformly.
  • Whereas the deterministic model fixed production emissions at 45 kg {{CO2e}} / head / year, the probabilistic model varies it uniformly from 20x to 50x less than the reduced methane {{CO2e}}.
  • Whereas the deterministic model ignored income tax, the probabilistic model includes recouping it at a rate of 25%.
The probabilistic model estimates the cost per tonne {{CO2}}e abated over modelled period to lie in the range 188.99 - 200.08 (CAD / t{{CO2}}e) with 95% confidence. This is lower than the deterministic estimate of $222/t {{CO2}}e in the previous post because of the recognition of income tax revenue. Appendix 1 provides a complete listing of modelling changes.

The range of cost per ton is relatively tight because despite many sources of uncertainty, the only one that affects this particular statistic is the uncertainty in production emissions.

NIR Prediction with the Scaling Model (Pre-NIR-2025-m04)

One possible use of PlanZero's predictive probabilistic models is to anticipate the values in National [Greenhouse Gas] Inventory Reports (NIRs) before they are released. If a model tends to be good at anticipating previously-released NIRs based on data available at previous points in time, we might believe it will be good at anticipating not-yet-released NIRs based on data available now. So far PlanZero includes just one NIR-prediction challenge (Pre-NIR-2025-m04, described in this post) which is about predicting the last year of NIR-2025 data (calendar year 2023), 4 months ahead of publication.

Since the Scaling model introduced in this post is a predictive model, it can be evaluated according to the Pre-NIR-2025-m04 protocol and evaluation metrics. The national-total-level results are included below, the full sector-by-sector scores can be found on the Scaling page

Name Short Description weighted KL-divergence (lower is better, zero is perfect)}
Scaling Hypothetical scaling of emission-reduction measures within an otherwise non-time-varying economy 3.376
Static Normals (data-cutoff: 2024-12-31) Very simple baseline model - non-time-varying estimates 3.367

The Scaling model is slightly worse at predicting NIR data compared with the Static Normals model upon which it is based. The slight deterioration in prediction quality is expected based on the difference in modelling approach, which trades off the fitting of overall enteric emissions with static normals, with static normal models instead for two contributing variables: the cattle populations (by type) and emission factors (per cattle type). The Scaling model introduces these variables so that the Bovaer strategy has something to work on (the emission factors). A side-effect is that there are more constraints to satisfy (Statistics Canada publishes cattle population data, the NIR publishes emission factors for cattle, and the NIR also includes provincial and national enteric emission totals). Also this model assumes cattle are the only livestock type contributing enteric emissions, which is about 96-97% true, but not 100% true. For all of these reasons, if not more, the resulting posterior estimate for the NIR totals isn't as accurate as the one in the Static Normals model. Predictive accuracy isn't the strengh of static normals models generally, so a slight degradation doesn't bother me. I expect a jump in predictive accuracy across almost every sector when replacing the static normals baseline with an autoregressive model, which I hope to report with a post later this fall. For now, the details of modelling in the Scaling model are explained in Appendix 2: Probabilistic Modelling of Cattle Populations and Enteric Fermentation Emission Factors.

Conclusion

This post has introduced a probabilistic "Scaling" model, which was created by combining the static normals probabilistic model with new probabilistic versions of the modelling elements used previously to estimate the impact of Bovaer adoption. The PlanZero pages for probabilistic models now feature strategies and barriers for models that have those sorts of elements. This site also now includes pages for the strategies and barrier elements in a model, which serve to organize the logic and computations associated with each model. For example, the strategy page for "Scale Bovaer" currently features the figures above, and the "Bovaer Adoption Limit" barrier page describes and shows the assumption that just-over 2.5% to just-under 10% of farmers will start feeding Bovaer per year. The terms "strategy" and "barrier" will be explained in more detail in an upcoming post documenting PlanZero's computational modelling framework.

My ambition for PlanZero in the not-too-distant future is to improve the Scaling model iteratively, first by the overall use of an autoregressive baseline model instead of the static normals, and then by sector-by-sector replacements of the generic statistical baseline with bespoke sector models that incorporate additional data sources and strategies. However, before making these iterative improvements I would like to finalize the glossary and perform significant clean-up and refactoring of PlanZero's source code so that those iterative changes will be easier and more systematic.

Until then,

- James Bergstra

Appendix 1: New and Modified Barrier Elements to Model Bovaer Adoption Probabilistically

The Scaling Model is identical to the Static Normals model (specifically with Dec 2024 data cutoff) in every sector except "Enteric Fermentation" and "Other Product Manufacture and Use". This appendix enumerates new barrier elements in the Scaling model, relative to the Static Normals model, which combine to estimate the changes in those two sectors. The scaling model totally replaces the estimation of Enteric Fermentation and adds a new term that raises emissions in "Other Product Manufacture and Use".

The new barrier elements are:

  • Cattle Population Static Normal
  • Bovaer Adoption Limit
  • Bovaer Production Emission Factors

Cattle Population Static Normal

Link to barrier page: Cattle Population Static Normal

This barrier element makes static normal estimates of cattle populations and enteric fermentation emission factors to explain three things:

  1. cattle counts
  2. NIR2025 enteric fermentation emission factors
  3. NIR2025 overall enteric fermentation emissions (including all livestock)
Estimates are made for each of 8 cattle populations (beef cows, dairy cows, beef heifers for replacement, beef heifers for slaughter, dairy heifers, calves, steers, and bulls), and each province and territory.

The Static Normals model only estimated the overall enteric fermentation emission. In this model, this element estimates it instead as a sum-product of the number of cattle of each type, and the emission factors of each type. Details of the probabilistic model are provided below in Appendix 2: Probabilistic Modelling of Cattle Populations and Enteric Fermentation Emission Factors.

Bovaer Adoption Limit

Link to barrier page: Barrier Adoption Limit

Previously when modelling Bovaer deterministically, this element assumed that no more than 5.0 percent / year of farmers would switch to administering Bovaer in any given year, but that adoption could ultimately rise to 99.0%, (the 1% remainder being organic farmers who can't adopt it). Here, when used in a probabilistic model, this element assumes instead that 2.5-10.0% of farmers will switch to Bovaer in any given year.

Bovaer Production Emission Factors

Link to barrier page: Bovaer Production Emission Factors

When used in deterministic models, this element assumes an extra 45.0 kg {{CO2}} / head / year in the "Other Product Manufacture and Use" sector, in order to account for producing and distributing the Bovaer feed additive.

In the probabilistic version of this barrier, the amount is based instead on a "20-50x" reduction in CO2e compared with methane emitted from cattle. This was the literal suggestion from an LLM-AI chat earlier this year, which was the original source for the 45.0 kg {{CO2}} / head / year value. For each sampled scenario, a reduction factor is sampled uniformly from 20 to 50.

This barrier does not reflect manufacturer or 3rd party estimates of emission rates nor does it include any change in production emission efficiency over time.

Bovaer Monitoring

Link to barrier page: Bovaer Monitoring

As before, this modelling element assumes administering and monitoring costs 1000.0 CAD / farm / year for paperwork and 3000.0 CAD / farm / year for on-site inspection. When used in probabilistic modelling, this element furthermore estimates that these amounts are paid in labour costs, from which income tax is collected back at a rate of 25%.

Appendix 2: Probabilistic Modelling of Cattle Populations and Enteric Fermentation Emission Factors

The Static Normals model (specifically with Dec 2024 data cutoff) modelled every sector's national and regional (provincial and territorial) emissions in the same way, described in Appendix 2 of the post introducing that model. The Scaling model handles Enteric Emissions differently, in order to introduce variables that could be acted-upon to simulate the effects of scaling Bovaer adoption.

Also, in order to make reasonable inferences of the cattle-type populations and of their respective emission factors, this post draws on more data sources. Statistics Canada publishes national and regional cattle populations for each January 1 and July 1 since the 1930s. That data was described and first used in PlanZero in this post, which explored the causes of enteric fermentation emissions. The NIR-2025 document also publishes the emission factors it has used for cattle and other livestock for each year covered, from 1990-2023.

As noted in the previous post on enteric emissions, cattle are responsible for 96-97% of the national total in that sector, with the rest coming from other livestock types. This model assumes they contribute 100%. Future modelling work could compensate for this 3-4% gap in a few ways, but it isn't done yet.

Formally, the enteric fermentation emissions, and cattle populations, and emissions factors in the Scaling model are estimated as the posterior distributions in this model after observing data up until Dec 31, 2024 from the three data sources above.
Unknown Variable and Distribution Description
{{latex("\\phi_{(c,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathrm{Lognormal}(-1, 2)")}} Scaled cattle headcount means, by cattle type {{latex("c")}} and region {{latex("r")}}. The scaling factor was the inverse of the largest recorded cattle-type headcount in the historical data, so that the scaled, observed cattle headcounts would lie between 0 and 1.
{{latex("\\beta_{(c,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathrm{Lognormal}(-1, 1)")}} Scaled cattle headcount standard deviations, by cattle type {{latex("c")}} and region {{latex("r")}}.
{{latex("h_{(c,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\phi_{(c, r)}, \\beta_{(c, r)})")}} Observable scaled cattle headcount, by cattle type {{latex("c")}} and region {{latex("r")}}.
{{latex("\\gamma_{(c)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(A, B^2)")}} A latent emission factor {{latex("c")}} was defined for each cattle type, and used across regions. It was modelled as coming a-priori from a normal distribution around {{latex("A")}}, the approximate NIR-published means over time, and {{latex("B^2")}}, the approximate variance over time.
{{latex("f_{(c)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\gamma_{(c)}, 20)")}} Observable emission factor, defined for each cattle type.
{{latex("\\sigma_{(r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathrm{Lognormal}(-1, 0.7)")}} A-priori, the variance in scaled regional emissions (looking across years) is not expected to be super-tiny, nor much larger than 1.
{{latex("\\sigma_{()} ~\\sim~ \\mathrm{Lognormal}(-1, 0.7)")}} A-priori, the variance in scaled national emissions (looking across years) is not expected to be super-tiny, nor much larger than 1.
{{latex("X_{(r,t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}( \\sum_c h_{(c,r)} f_{(c)}, \\sigma_{(r)}^2)")}} Observable model-estimated scaled NIR emissions for the region ({{latex("r")}}), and annual time period ({{latex("t")}}). Note that the distribution is "static", in that it is the same for all annual time periods.
{{latex("Y_{(t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}( \\sum_{r \\in \\mathrm{regions}} \\sum_c h_{(c,r)} f_{(c)}, \\sigma_{(s,g)}^2)")}} Observable model-estimated scaled national-total emissions for enteric fermentation emissions (methane). Note that the distribution is static, in that it is the same for all annual time periods.

Posterior estimates of the cattle population and emission factors were obtained using NumPyro's NUTS sampler and drawing 500 samples after warming up for 250. Cattle population counts were adapted for inference by averaging January and July headcounts to get annual headcounts.
