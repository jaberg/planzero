Overview

As of this post, PlanZero models are large {{siteref("Probabilistic Model", "probabilistic models")|safe}}. They encompass all of the emissions of the Canada's recent annual National Inventory Reports, and this site aims, in future, to extend the models with a growing number of driving factors of those emissions. There are 71 sectors in ICCC's interpretation of the IPCC protocol, and 173 non-neglibible sector-GHG combinations. Each of the 173 sector-GHG combinations is the sum of contributions across 12 or 13 regions (provinces and territories) depending on the year (the inventory starts in 1990 but Nunavut was created in 1998). For each sector-GHG-region combination, the NIRs include emissions data for some or all years in a reporting period starting in 1990, and PlanZero's NIR models extend those time series with forecasts until 2050. The models therefore contemplate a total of 137,189 emission amounts (173 sector-GHG combinations times 13 regions times 61 years). PlanZero models also contemplate variables beyond emissions amounts, such as, in the case of the Scaling model: the number of cattle of various types in each region over time, the amount of money available to fund Bovaer adoption, the emission factors of specific cattle types in the modelling of enteric fermentation emissions, and many more. As PlanZero grows to model emissions drivers and interventions relating to specific sectors beyond enteric fermentation, the number of variables in the model will grow. This post is about how PlanZero models can be understood in terms of smaller, more-comprehensible elements.

PlanZero models manage semantic complexity by building up from {{siteref("Model Element", "elements")|safe}} ({{siteref("Structural Element", "structural elements")|safe}} and {{siteref("Strategy Element", "strategy elements")|safe}}) and organizing those elements into {{siteref("Inference Group", "inference groups")|safe}}. Each element provides logic to define one or more variables in the model. An element might might define a variable in terms of numbers (e.g. a Statistics Canada table) or in terms of a distribution (e.g. the probabilistic interpretation of emissions from NIR-2025). Most, if not all, of the elements in a PlanZero model are structural elements. These elements define necessary relationships between variables in the model. Some models, such as the Scaling model, include strategy elements, which offer optional variable definitions. Some elements are meant to work together; they wouldn't mean the right thing or wouldn't work at all without the presence of the others. Such a subset of elements in a PlanZero model is called an inference group. An inference group is the smallest piece of a PlanZero model that can be evaluated on its own, independently of the rest of the model.

The screenshot below, from the new Model Explorer's Overview tab, shows the Scaling model's inference group for Enteric Fermentation and Other Product Manufacturing. This inference group includes elements related to hypothesized dynamics of Bovaer adoption. On the model explorer page, mouseover effects provide more information about the meaning of the model elements, and the roles of the variables involved in the connections between the model elements. The number-cells in the table reflect connectivity between model elements; for example, the blue-shaded "1" in row E, column D, means there is one (1) variable that is defined by model element E (Bovaer Farm Subsidy), and accessed by the model element D (Bovaer Adoption Limit). Hovering a mouse pointer over that blue-shaded "1" in the model explorer page would reveal the roles of that one connecting variable in both model elements, so you could see that the variable in question is the number of farms in the subsidy program. Refer to Appendix 1: Model Structure for more detail on the compositional elements of PlanZero models.

The model explorer also features a second tab, a "Trace" tab, that shows the interactions between selected model elements in more detail, including the use of variables to pass information between model elements, and use of those variables in specific computational subphases (screenshot below).

In the Trace tab screenshot (above), variables are defined from top to bottom, following the direction of the edges in the graph (with the exception of dashed backward edges from "next" to "this" nodes, see Appendix 2). The three computational subphases are:

  1. Preparation computations (blue nodes, upper area) define variables that don't depend on any recursive annual definition
  2. Annual step computations (teal nodes, middle area) define variables that must be woven in among recursive annual definitions
  3. Post-processing computations (purple nodes, lower area) define variables that are not needed until after recursive annual definitions
Appendix 2: Semantics and Evaluation provides more detail about how to read such graphs, as well as how model construction relates to probabilistic inference and data-fitting. For this overview, suffice it to say that the Trace tab shows the interactions between selected model elements and the variables accessed and defined by those elements.

The new modelling terms introduced in this post (and other terms that have come up so far in the course of developing PlanZero) are included as Appendix 3: Glossary Snapshot, which is a snapshot of a new Glossary page. Appendix 1: Model Structure

{{siteref("PlanZero_Model", "PlanZero models")|safe}} can be understood at the highest level as comprising:

  • Model Variables: specific numbers and distributions over possible numbers
  • Model Elements: mathematical relationships between variables
Model variables represent numbers. A variable can stand for a single number, such as a mean emission amount in the Static Normals) model. A variable can stand for annual time series, such as Canada's total national emissions since 1990 according to NIR-2025. A variable can also correspond to multi-dimensional tabular data such a matrix or a higher-dimentional table with 3 or more indices. Sometimes variables correspond to specific, known numbers (known variable), and sometimes they correspond to a probability distribution over possible numbers (unknown variable). Model elements implement the definitions of variables, and thereby establish the mathematical relationships between model variables. Model elements, and the model variables to which they refer, taken together, make up a PlanZero model.

{{siteref("Model_Variable", "Model variables")|safe}} in a PlanZero model can be classified according to (a) the knowledge about them in the model and (b) the availability of observation data. {{siteref("Known_Variable", "Known variables")|safe}} are associated with numbers that are known at the outset of modelling, such as data from statistical reports, and physical constants. If a variable depends in a known way on known variables, then it too is known. If, instead, a variable is modelled as a probability distribution, then it is an {{siteref("Unknown_Variable", "unknown variable")|safe}}. Every variable that is modelled as depending on that variable is also considered to be unknown. If we have data that we model as being a sample from the distribution of an unknown variable (or if we have a distribution from which to draw such data), then that unknown variable can be furthermore called an {{siteref("Observed_Variable", "observed variable")|safe}}. If it isn't observed, an unknown variable is considered to be {{siteref("Unobserved_Variable", "unobserved")|safe}}. The {{siteref("Joint_Distribution", "joint distribution")|safe}} over all of the unknown variables in a model is the probabilistic model induced by a PlanZero model.

Emission Variables

PlanZero models always have a full set of {{siteref("Emission_Variables", "emissions variables")|safe}}, covering each year (from 1990 to some forecasting horizon such as 2050 or 2100), in units of ktCO2e, of each gas, from each region (province and territory), associated with each of the 71 NIR-2025 reporting sectors. The estimates from a given model may be more or less uncertain, but they must all be present in order for the pages on the site to render correctly. The visualizations of these estimates provide the main graphics for model summaries and per-sector summary pages featured on the site. I imagine at some point, a future NIR will change the reporting taxonomy from the 71 sectors of the NIR-2025 to some other set. At such a time, PlanZero modelling will need to adapt to the new taxonomy in order to continue forecasting NIR data. The NIR-2025 and Static-Normals models have only these emissions variables as modelling outputs.

Cashflow Variables

PlanZero models that contain strategies (more on strategies, below) may model the movement of money with {{siteref("Cashflow_Variables", "cashflow variables")|safe}}. To use our running example of Bovaer, the Scale-Bovaer strategy works by defining a variable that represents the money available to fund the program to have a large available amount. Cash, in PlanZero models, is modelled as flowing among a fixed set of economic entities called is a concept of {{siteref("Special_Project_Entity", "Special Project Entities (SPEs)")|safe}}. SPEs are abstract economic entities with cash balances. SPEs represent non-overlapping sets of actual or hypothetical individuals and corporate entities, such as e.g. "Farms administering Bovaer", "Farms not administering Bovaer", and a hypothetical federal "Bovaer Subsidy Program". Since PlanZero models only the flow of money from one SPE into another one, the annual sum of all cashflow is always zero. It is by combining cashflow estimates with emissions estimates that the PlanZero build system calculates a strategy's {{siteref("Emission_Reduction_Efficiency", "efficiency of emissions reduction")|safe}}, in terms of e.g. cost per t{{CO2e}} abated.

Other Variables

Beyond emissions and cashflow variables, A planzero model can include various other forecasts of contributing factors, or latent explanatory variables, in order to estimate emissions and cashflow. Such quantities can be modelled in various ways, and are expected to be documented where they arise by the model elements that define them (see below). Other variables are necessary in PlanZero models, they are simply not directly used in standard visualizations of models or strategies, or to compute standard model metrics. Future work may add support for visualizing some/all of these so-called other variables to the site, but as of writing that functionality is not on the site.

Model Elements

To summarize this first section on model variables outputs, PlanZero models (1) always estimate emissions, and always do so in terms of emissions per year, per sector, per gas, and per provincial or territorial region; (2) sometimes estimate cashflows, but if they do so, they do so in a standard way in terms of a fixed set of SPEs; and (3) typically include other estimates as well, but not in a standardized way that qualify such things as full-model-level outputs. The next sections on strategy elements and structural elements explain how all of these variables are modelled.

These element types are adapted from concepts from strategic management, in consideration of Canada as an organization. In particular, relative to " The Executive's Guide to Facilitating Strategy " by Michael Wilkinson (2011), outcomes and metrics would be key performance indicators (KPIs) related to "Goals" (broad aims that define accomplishment of a mission of emissions reduction); structural elements would be a formalization of "Barriers" (existing or potential challenges that hinder the achievement of one or more objectives); and strategy elements represent "Strategies" (broad activities required to achieve an objective).

Structural Elements

Structural elements are fragments of modelling logic that, taken together, represent the assumptions about what variables are in the model (other than the emissions variables, which are always present) and how those variables are related to one another, either deterministically or probabilistically. A deterministic relationship between variables A and B might be that A is twice B. A probabilistic relationship between variables A and B might be that A is Normally-distributed about a mean that is twice B, with such-and-such variance. (In either case, B might be known exactly, or it may be an unknown variable with its own distribution under the model.) Structural elements are required, and make up most if not all of the elements of a full model: the Static-Normals model, for example, is made up entirely of structural elements; the Scaling model, as of writing, is made up of hundreds of structural elements and a single strategy element.

Structural elements do many kinds of work, such as e.g.

  • loading data as observations of unknown variables
  • defining the priors on static normals in the Static Normals model
  • computing the weighted-KL divergence metric from NIR-2025 actuals
  • defining hypothetical dynamics of Bovaer uptake across Canadian farms
  • summing up emissions across regions, sectors, and gases
Any set of structural elements that defines all of the necessary emissions can be a valid PlanZero model. Two of the models currently listed on the PlanZero models page are composed entirely of structural elements: NIR-2025 and Static Normals. Models pages on the PlanZero site generally feature a listing of their key structural elements, and links to per-structural-element pages documenting those structural elements. The Scaling model has one additional type of element, a strategy element; strategy elements are the subject of the next section.

Strategies: broad activities that could be pursued

Strategy elements are elements that are meant to be ablated (individually removed) in order to compare model outputs with and without the presence of the strategy element. Strategies are indended to explore opportunities for capital, labour, and technology to reduce emissions. In future, PlanZero could be perhaps be extended to evaluate strategies in terms of attracting investment, creating durable employment, affecting land use, or affecting trade, sovereignty, and other matters of policy, but those things are not currently modelled. PlanZero currently features just a single strategy element, which is to fund a hypothetical federal Bovaer subsidy program. When the hypothetical program SPE is funded, then pre-existing structural elements draw funds from the program to e.g. Bovaer purchase, farm subsidies, and monitoring programs. When the hypothetical program SPE is not funded, then the pre-existing structural elements have no effect.

Strategy elements are similar in nature to structural elements, but they must be defined such that the model is still valid without their presence. So for example, they can't define emission variables directly. They must work by offering definitions for variables that would otherwise assume default values. In the case of the Bovaer strategy, the default balance for the hypothetical program is 0, and the strategy implements a supposition that it were sufficiently funded to support as many farmers as would opt-in.

PlanZero model pages list the strategies in a model, and link to per-strategy pages that document each strategy and its impact on the model outputs. The impact of a strategy is determined by evaluating the full model with the strategy included (which is the same for all strategies), and then again without including it (which is unique for each strategy), and comparing the two sets of results. PlanZero performs this comparison (called {{siteref("Ablative Analysis")|safe}}) automatically to produce charts and analysis for each strategy page.

Inference Groups

PlanZero models are too large for a direct application of NumPyro's standard inference algorithms. At the same time, it is also true that PlanZero models, to date, comprise many independent parts; each of these parts is of a suitable size for standard inference algorithms. For example, Oil and Gas Extraction emissions are independent of Venting emissions in the Static Normals model. In order to leverage this independence and use standard inference algorithms, PlanZero models are constructed with a concept of {{siteref("Inference Group", "Inference Groups")|safe}}, which are non-overlapping sets of structural and strategy elements, each of which defines a submodel suitable for independent inference. Inference in such submodels is meant to take a few minutes (for each inference group).

Model Explorer

While inference groups are necessary for computational practicality, they also provide a communication benefit: a model's inference groups can fit in a readable list, even if it's a bit long (e.g. 100-200 groups). Browsing through inference groups lets a reader connect from a full model, down to the individual model elements, of which there may be thousands in total. The PlanZero now includes a Model Explorer page, which uses inference groups to expose the elements of a model. The "Overview" tab on the model explorer shows a tabular view of the connectivity between elements in a model. The model explorer also include a "Trace" tab, that shows the computational relationships between variables and model elements, but to explain what's shown in that tab will require terminology from the next section, about model computation and variable types.

Appendix 2: Model Evaluation

Model evaluation is the computational task of determining a model's outputs (the variables outlined in the first section of Appendix 1.) PlanZero models are evaluated in three phases (prior, inference and posterior) as explained in the next section. Within each phase there are are three computational sub-phases (preparation, annual scan, and post-processing), and those sub-phases help explain the three kinds of variable that PlanZero models can have (general, annual, and carry variables).

Phases of model evaluation: prior, inference, and posterior

In future, a third Model Explorer tab or panel element may offer to show the numbers associated with each variable node in the Trace tab, but that functionality is not yet implemented. explains, in general terms and at a high level, how PlanZero models produce the numbers that are featured on the site. PlanZero models are evaluated in three phases, typical of probabilistic models: Prior, Inference, and Posterior. The numbers on the site are from the Posterior phase. Within each phase are three computational subphases of model construction, which allow model elements to share variable definitions while defining as many years' worth of modelled variables as are required, while also taking advantage of vectorized math libraries to perform the necessary computations quickly. Data flow through variables and computational subphases can now now visualized through a second tab, a Model Explorer Trace tab. It can visualize model element interactions such as the ones in the screenshot below: Such terms relate to the phases of computation in the model (e.g. prior and posterior phases of Bayesian inference) the sub-phases of computation within each phase (e.g. before, during, and after a recursive computation over the years of the modelled period) and the types of variable that can be accommodated within this computational framework (e.g. fixed-shape variables, variables that are defined for every year, and running statistics). Appendix 2 also explains terms relating to what PlanZero calls aspects of variables which relate to if (and how) an [unknown] variable should be considered to be observed, for the purpose of Bayesian inference. PlanZero's models page has been updated and consolidated so that there are no longer two subsections (there used to be "simulation-based models" and "probabilistic Models" subsections) there is just one list of [probabilistic] models:

  • NIR2025, the probabilistic interpretation of the NIR-2025 data
  • Static Normals, a baseline probabilistic model implementing an assumption that there will be no long-term trends in any of the NIR emissions sectors
  • Scaling, the model that is intended to include multiple scalable technologies, but currently includes just a hypothetical Bovaer program.
The so-called "simulation-based" models have been removed because they were redundant with the probabilistic models. What used to be simulation-based models were, conceptually, a restricted special case of probabilistic models. Furthermore, the way they were implemented was not amenable to Bayesian inference, so they've been replaced with fully probabilistic ones.

The first phase starts by loading data and drawing random samples from any independent unknown variables, and then proceeds to compute or sample variables that depend on those variables, and then variables that depend on those variables and so on until all variables have some value. This is called the Prior phase, and even though PlanZero models are large, it can be completed in a few seconds.

After the prior phase has completed, some analysis occurs around variables with both probability distributions and data, called {{siteref("Observed Variable", "observed variables")|safe}}. For each observed variable, PlanZero estimates the probability of the data according to the model. This is especially important in cases where the probability according to the model actually depends on other variables in the model, which have their own probabilities. Typically the parameters of the distribution over data (a) were themselves chosen randomly as part of the prior calculation, (b) don't make the data very probable, and (c) are hardly any more probable than some other parameter values that could have been chosen almost as easily, and which would have made the data appear far more probable. {{siteref("Bayesian Inference")|safe}} is about sampling, with the benefit of data, parameter values governing observed values that strike a balance between being probable themselves (according to the prior distribution) and making the data associated with observed variables also probable. PlanZero uses NumPyro (a probabilistic programming system) to compute samples that implement Bayesian inference of the {{siteref("Unobserved Variable", "unobserved variables")|safe}} in PlanZero models. In contrast with the computation of the Prior phase, which takes a few seconds, this inference of posterior samples can take hours for e.g. 500 samples. PlanZero implements strategies for caching and piecewise computation to prevent this computation from taking longer, or from being repeated unnecessarily. See NumPyro documentation for an introduction to the inference algorithms used in this phase.

The final phase of PlanZero model evaluation is called the {{siteref("Posterior Phase")|safe}}, which is a re-computation of the whole model for each of the e.g. 500 inferred values of unobserved variables. The posterior phase involves recomputation of values for observed variables, the semantics of which are: What is a plausible value for this observed variable, given all of the observations (with some amount of noise, which may or may not have been estimated) and any inferred values of governing model parameters? Or, simply: What does the model think the variable was? This computation is currently memory-intensive because it involves computing statistics over e.g. 500 possible values of every variable in the already-large model, but larger models could be accommodated by a more careful sequencing of the computation of statistics. Whereas prior computations take seconds, and inference computations take hours, posterior computations take a few minutes. The result of posterior computations are the statistics displayed on the site: the upper and lower quantiles of emission amounts, strategy costs, and so on are all calculated across the e.g. 500 plausible values associated with whatever is the relevant model output.

Computational subphases of model construction

There are three computational subphases of model construction, based around however many years are being modelled. Model elements can participate in any or all of the following model-construction sub-phases:

  • preparation: computation before any of the annual steps
  • annual steps: computation for each modelled year
  • post-processing: computation after the last annual step
Models are constructed by first doing all the preparation (of all the model elements), then doing some computation for each modelled year (giving every model element a chance to do something on each year), then finally giving each element a chance to do some post-processing computation. At each step of computation, the model elements can access variables that have already been defined, and the result of their computation is to define new variables. Eventually all the model elements have done all of their computations, and all of the model's variables are defined. This is how models are constructed in the Prior and Posterior phases. As was described above, Inference is a bit different because Inference Groups are evaluated independently; in the case of Inference, it is just each Inference Group that is constructed in this way, not the whole model.

General, annual, and carry variables

The nature of the computational subphases of model construction induce three types of variable:

  • general: a variable whose array size is independent of the number of years, and whose value does not depend on the year
  • annual: a variable whose array size is the number of years
  • carry: a variable whose array size is independent of the number of years, whose value does depend on the year
General variables are perhaps the simplest: they have a certain size and value. When a model element loads up data about a certain sector to define a variable, that is naturally a "general" variable. A general variable can be just a single number (a scalar) or an array of any number of dimensions (n-dimenional array). Annual variables must be at least one-dimensional, but they can be n-dimensional too. The first dimension of an annual variable allows it to provide a value for each modelled year. Annual variables are natural for e.g. the emissions predicted for each certain sector-gas-region combination. Carry variables are natural for recursively-defined variables. For example, the number of farmers administering Bovaer in a given year is naturally defined as the number from the previous year plus or minus some delta.

There are various constraints on how the computational subphases can access and define various variable types. For example, an annual step cannot define a general variable (the value of a general variable can't depend on the year), and a preparation computation cannot access the final value of a carry variable (it won't be defined until after the last annual step, which comes after preparation). Still, the modelling framework seems flexible enough, and the constraints ensure that PlanZero can run all of the model phases and subphases in a correct, predictable way, no matter what elements make up any given model.
