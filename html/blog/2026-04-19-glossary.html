Overview

As the nature of PlanZero models continues to evolve, this post

  1. finally announces the addition of the PlanZero glossary to the site (the glossary has been present for some time), and
  2. introduces a new interactive model explorer page to the site, which uses the model concepts introduecd in this post to provide new options for visualizing PlanZero's large probabilistic models of national emissions and possible technology roadmaps.
With the underlying changes accompanying this post, PlanZero has dropped the notion of so-called "simulation-based" models and is now entirely dedicated to probabilistic modelling. This post explains what PlanZero models are, adds the necessary new terminology to the glossary, and provides the model-explorer to query PlanZero models in those terms.

PlanZero models are large probabilistic models. They encompass all of the emissions of the Canada's recent annual National Inventory Reports, and this site aims, in future, to extend the models with a growing number of driving factors of those emissions. There are 71 sectors in ICCC's interpretation of the IPCC protocol, and 173 non-neglibible sector-GHG combinations. Each of the 173 sector-GHG combinations is the sum of contributions across 12 or 13 regions (provinces and territories) depending on the year (the inventory starts in 1990 but Nunavut was created in 1998). For each sector-GHG-region combination, the NIRs include emissions data for some or all years in a reporting period starting in 1990, and PlanZero's NIR models extend those time series with forecasts until 2050. The models therefore contemplate a total of 137,189 emission amounts (173 sector-GHG combinations times 13 regions times 61 years). PlanZero models also contemplate variables beyond emissions amounts, such as, in the case of the Scaling model: the number of cattle of various types in each region over time, the amount of money available to fund Bovaer adoption, the emission factors of specific cattle types in the modelling of enteric fermentation emissions, and many more. As PlanZero grows to model emissions drivers and interventions relating to specific sectors beyond enteric fermentation, the number of variables in the model will grow.

PlanZero manages the semantic complexity of models by defining models from pieces (structural elements and strategy elements) and organizing those pieces into inference groups. Each element provides logic to define one or more variables in the model. An element might might define a variable in terms of numbers (e.g. a Statistics Canada table) or in terms of a distribution (e.g. the probabilistic interpretation of emissions from NIR-2025). The vast majority of elements in a PlanZero model are so-called structural elements because they define necessary relationships between variables in the model. Some models, such as the Scaling model, include one or more strategy elements, which offer possible-but-not-necessary variable definitions. When a model includes strategy elements, PlanZero estimates the impact of each strategy by evaluating model variants with and without including the strategy. (The one and only strategy element implemented to date is the strategy of funding a government program to subsidize the administration of a feed additive such as Bovaer, but as an illustrative element it serves its purpose.) Inference groups are simply sets of elements that define some emissions variables, and don't rely on variables defined by elements outside the group. An inference group can be evaluated and investigated independently of other groups, which helps with the computation and visualization of the overall model. For a more detailed overview of PlanZero's semantic modelling framework, including also the outcomes (so far: emissions and costs) and the metrics used to evaluate models, see Appendix 1: Semantic Modelling Framework.

PlanZero's updated computational framework is also apparent here on the site to some degree, and those terms that come through are explained in Appendix 2: , as well as added to the glossary. Such terms relate to the phases of computation in the model (e.g. prior and posterior phases of Bayesian inference) the sub-phases of computation within each phase (e.g. before, during, and after a recursive computation over the years of the modelled period) and the types of variable that can be accommodated within this computational framework (e.g. fixed-shape variables, variables that are defined for every year, and running statistics). Appendix 2 also explains terms relating to what PlanZero calls aspects of variables which relate to if (and how) an [unknown] variable should be considered to be observed, for the purpose of Bayesian inference. Finally Appendix 3 includes a snapshot of the glossary at time of writing.

PlanZero's models page has been updated and consolidated so that there are no longer two subsections (there used to be "simulation-based models" and "probabilistic Models" subsections) there is just one list of [probabilistic] models:

  • NIR2025, the probabilistic interpretation of the NIR-2025 data
  • Static Normals, a baseline probabilistic model implementing an assumption that there will be no long-term trends in any of the NIR emissions sectors
  • Scaling, the model that is intended to include multiple scalable technologies, but currently includes just a hypothetical Bovaer program.
The so-called "simulation-based" models have been removed because they were redundant with the probabilistic models. What used to be simulation-based models were, conceptually, a restricted special case of probabilistic models. Furthermore, the way they were implemented was not amenable to Bayesian inference, so they've been replaced with fully probabilistic ones.

Appendix 1: Semantic Modelling Framework

PlanZero models can be understood at the highest level as comprising two types of thing:

  • Model Variables: specific numbers and distributions over possible numbers
  • Model Elements: mathematical relationships between variables
Model variables represent numbers. A variable can stand for a single number, such as a mean emission amount in the Static Normals) model. A variable can stand for annual time series, such as Canada's total national emissions since 1990 according to NIR-2025. A variable can also correspond to multi-dimensional tabular data such a matrix or a higher-dimentional table with 3 or more indices. Sometimes variables correspond to specific, known numbers (known variable), and sometimes they correspond to a probability distribution over possible numbers (unknown variable). Model elements implement the definitions of variables, and thereby establish the mathematical relationships between model variables. Model elements, and the model variables to which they refer, taken together, make up a PlanZero model.

Model Variables

The purpose of PlanZero models is to model three kinds of quantities:

  • [Always] Annual emission amounts associated with sector-gas-region combinations
  • [Sometimes] Annual cashflows associated with Special Project Entities (SPEs)
  • [Typically] Miscellaneous other quantities of relevance to specific sector-gas-region models

Annual emissions estimates are what got PlanZero modelling started, starting with the NIR-2025 data. PlanZero models are designed to make estimates for each year (from 1990 to some forecasting horizon such as 2050 or 2100), in units of ktCO2e, of each gas, from each region (province and territory), associated with each of the 71 NIR-2025 reporting sectors. The estimates from a given model may be more or less uncertain, but they'll all be there. The visualizations of these estimates provide the main graphics for model summaries and per-sector summary pages featured on the site. I imagine at some point, a future NIR will change the reporting taxonomy from the 71 sectors of the NIR-2025 to some other set. At such a time, PlanZero modelling will need to adapt to the new taxonomy in order to continue forecasting NIR data. The NIR-2025 and Static-Normals models have only these emissions variables as modelling outputs.

PlanZero models that contain strategies (more on strategies, below) explore how the movement of money might affect emissions. In these models (such as the Scaling model) there is a concept of Special Project Entities (SPEs), which are abstract economic entities with cash balances. PlanZero models may estimate movement of money between SPEs as a distinct kind of modelling output beyond emissions amounts. SPEs represent non-overlapping sets of actual or hypothetical individuals and corporate entities, such as e.g. "Farms administering Bovaer", "Farms not administering Bovaer", and a hypothetical federal "Bovaer Subsidy Program". Since PlanZero models only the flow of money from one SPE into another one, the annual sum of all cashflow is always zero. Cashflow estimtes can be combined with emissions estimates to estimate a strategy's efficiency of emissions reduction, in terms of e.g. cost per t{{CO2e}} abated.

The final type of quantity, after emissions and cashflow, is, for lack of a better term: everything else. A planzero model can include various other forecasts of contributing factors, or latent explanatory variables, in order to estimate emissions and cashflow. Such quantities can be modelled in various ways, and are expected to be documented where they arise by the so-called structural elements and strategy elements that define them. Structural elements and strategy elements are the subjects of the next sections.

To summarize this first section on modelling outputs, PlanZero models (1) always estimate emissions, and always do so in terms of emissions per year, per sector, per gas, and per provincial or territorial region; (2) sometimes estimate cashflows, but if they do so, they do so in a standard way in terms of a fixed set of SPEs; and (3) typically include other estimates as well, but not in a standardized way that qualify such things as full-model-level outputs. The next sections on strategy elements and structural elements explain how all of these variables are modelled.

Model Elements

These element types are adapted from concepts from strategic management, in consideration of Canada as an organization. In particular, relative to " The Executive's Guide to Facilitating Strategy " by Michael Wilkinson (2011), outcomes and metrics would be key performance indicators (KPIs) related to "Goals" (broad aims that define accomplishment of a mission of emissions reduction); structural elements would be a formalization of "Barriers" (existing or potential challenges that hinder the achievement of one or more objectives); and strategy elements represent "Strategies" (broad activities required to achieve an objective).

Structural elements are fragments of modelling logic that, taken together, represent the assumptions about what variables are in the model (other than the emissions variables, which are always present) and how those variables are related to one another, either deterministically or probabilistically. A deterministic relationship between variables A and B might be that A is twice B. A probabilistic relationship between variables A and B might be that A is Normally-distributed about a mean that is twice B, with such-and-such variance. (In either case, B might be known exactly, or it may be an unknown variable with its own distribution under the model.) Structural elements are required, and make up most if not all of the elements of a full model: the Static-Normals model, for example, is made up entirely of structural elements; the Scaling model, as of writing, is made up of hundreds of structural elements and a single strategy element.

Structural elements do many kinds of work, such as e.g.

  • loading data as observations of unknown variables
  • defining the priors on static normals in the Static Normals model
  • computing the weighted-KL divergence metric from NIR-2025 actuals
  • defining hypothetical dynamics of Bovaer uptake across Canadian farms
  • summing up emissions across regions, sectors, and gases
Any set of structural elements that defines all of the necessary emissions can be a valid PlanZero model. Two of the models currently listed on the PlanZero models page are composed entirely of structural elements: NIR-2025 and Static Normals. Models pages on the PlanZero site generally feature a listing of their key structural elements, and links to per-structural-element pages documenting those structural elements. The Scaling model has one additional type of element, a strategy element; strategy elements are the subject of the next section.

Strategies: broad activities that could be pursued

Strategy elements are elements that are meant to be ablated (individually removed) in order to compare model outputs with and without the presence of the strategy element. Strategies are indended to explore opportunities for capital, labour, and technology to reduce emissions. In future, PlanZero could be perhaps be extended to evaluate strategies in terms of attracting investment, creating durable employment, affecting land use, or affecting trade, sovereignty, and other matters of policy, but those things are not currently modelled. PlanZero currently features just a single strategy element, which is to fund a hypothetical federal Bovaer subsidy program. When the hypothetical program SPE is funded, then pre-existing structural elements draw funds from the program to e.g. Bovaer purchase, farm subsidies, and monitoring programs. When the hypothetical program SPE is not funded, then the pre-existing structural elements have no effect.

Strategy elements are similar in nature to structural elements, but they must be defined such that the model is still valid without their presence. So for example, they can't define emission variables directly. They must work by offering definitions for variables that would otherwise assume default values. In the case of the Bovaer strategy, the default balance for the hypothetical program is 0, and the strategy implements a supposition that it were sufficiently funded to support as many farmers as would opt-in.

PlanZero model pages list the strategies in a model, and link to per-strategy pages that document each strategy and its impact on the model outputs. The impact of a strategy is determined by evaluating the full model with the strategy included (which is the same for all strategies), and then again without including it (which is unique for each strategy), and comparing the two sets of results. PlanZero performs this comparison (called {{siteref("Ablative Analysis")|safe}}) automatically to produce charts and analysis for each strategy page.

Appendix 2: Model Evaluation

Phases of model evaluation: prior, inference, and posterior

Model evaluation is the computational task of determining a model's outputs (the variables outlined in the first section of Appendix 1.) PlanZero models are evaluated in two phases. The first phase starts by loading data and drawing random samples from any independent unknown variables, and then proceeds to compute or sample variables that depend on those variables, and then variables that depend on those variables and so on until all variables have some value. This is called the Prior phase, and even though PlanZero models are large, it can be completed in a few seconds.

After the prior phase has completed, some analysis occurs around variables with both probability distributions and data, called {{siteref("Observed Variable", "observed variables")|safe}}. For each observed variable, PlanZero estimates the probability of the data according to the model. This is especially important in cases where the probability according to the model actually depends on other variables in the model, which have their own probabilities. Typically the parameters of the distribution over data (a) were themselves chosen randomly as part of the prior calculation, (b) don't make the data very probable, and (c) are hardly any more probable than some other parameter values that could have been chosen almost as easily, and which would have made the data appear far more probable. {{siteref("Bayesian Inference")|safe}} is about sampling, with the benefit of data, parameter values governing observed values that strike a balance between being probable themselves (according to the prior distribution) and making the data associated with observed variables also probable. PlanZero uses NumPyro (a probabilistic programming system) to compute samples that implement Bayesian inference of the {{siteref("Unobserved Variable", "unobserved variables")|safe}} in PlanZero models. In contrast with the computation of the Prior phase, which takes a few seconds, this inference of posterior samples can take hours for e.g. 500 samples. PlanZero implements strategies for caching and piecewise computation to prevent this computation from taking longer, or from being repeated unnecessarily. See NumPyro documentation for an introduction to the inference algorithms used in this phase.

The final phase of PlanZero model evaluation is called the {{siteref("Posterior Phase")|safe}}, which is a re-computation of the whole model for each of the e.g. 500 inferred values of unobserved variables. The posterior phase involves recomputation of values for observed variables, the semantics of which are: What is a plausible value for this observed variable, given all of the observations (with some amount of noise, which may or may not have been estimated) and any inferred values of governing model parameters? Or, simply: What does the model think the variable was? This computation is currently memory-intensive because it involves computing statistics over e.g. 500 possible values of every variable in the already-large model, but larger models could be accommodated by a more careful sequencing of the computation of statistics. Whereas prior computations take seconds, and inference computations take hours, posterior computations take a few minutes. The result of posterior computations are the statistics displayed on the site: the upper and lower quantiles of emission amounts, strategy costs, and so on are all calculated across the e.g. 500 plausible values associated with whatever is the relevant model output.

Inference Groups

PlanZero models are too large for a direct application of NumPyro's standard inference algorithms. At the same time, it is also true that PlanZero models, to date, comprise many independent parts, each of which is of a suitable size for standard inference algorithms. For example, in the Static Normals model, Oil and Gas Extraction emissions are independent of Venting emissions. But in that same model, modelled emissions from residential heating in British Columbia are not independent of modelled residential heating emissions from Newfoundland, because they both contribute to national total emissions data in NIR-2025 (in another model they might be further inter-related by e.g. the price of oil, but not in Static Normals). In order to leverage this independence and use standard inference algorithms, PlanZero models are constructed with a concept of {{siteref("Inference Group", "Inference Groups")|safe}}, which are sets of structural and strategy elements that are meant to define a submodel suitable for independent inference. Inference in such submodels (at least for models to date) typically takes a few minutes. Prior computations go through the whole model, inference computations go group-by-group, and posterior computations currently go through the whole model again, although they may go partly group-by-group in future.

The same define/access declarations can be explored interactively in the Model Explorer: use the overview matrix to see which elements depend on which, then click a cell to trace the individual variables and paths.

I haven't written about how PlanZero works before, and writing about how it worked made me think about how it worked, which made me realize that it should work better. Writing this glossary post, has, consequently, been one of the largest efforts of any post written to date. Among many changes (see {{siteref("GH")|safe}} {{siteref("PR")|safe}} #78) the most impactful is probably the conceptual clarification of Scenario and Simulation, which has led to improved visualizations, as well as the improved handling of KPIs (see below), which have enabled much better visualization of the impact of strategies. Visualizing and analyzing the impacts of strategies is one of the core capabilities that PlanZero needs to be good at, so I think the effort was time well spent. The rest of this post introduces the computational and modelling terms in narrative form, so that their entries in the glossary can be reasonably short, and link to this post for more context.
