Introduction

PlanZero's Modelling a Bovaer Strategy post from earlier this year estimated that a strategy of administering the feed additive Bovaer could remove up to 10 Mt{{CO2e}} annually at a cost of $222.94/t{{CO2e}}. Intuitively, the trouble with that estimate is that it is too precise to be credible. Formally, the trouble with that estimate is that it isn't really a {{siteref("Prediction", "prediction")|safe}} at all, it's just a number. Several subsequent posts have been developing a probabilistic modelling framework for PlanZero. The purpose of the probabilistic approach is to be able to state, with the support of appropriate data and assumptions, proper predictions (probability distributions) for such statistics / metrics as the {{siteref("Emission_Reduction_Efficiency", "emissions reduction efficiency")|safe}} of a strategy. A proper prediction regarding the impact of Bovaer would tell us whether that figure of $222.94 is accurate; it would tell us with what probability that figure is accurate to the nearest cent, the nearest dollar, the nearest one hundred dollars, and so on.

This post is about how PlanZero's probabilistic models can estimate the impact of a strategy and conclude instead that based on (a) the assumptions of the Static Normals model, and (b) additional structural assumptions related to a hypothetical Bovaer program, the cost per tonne {{CO2}}e abated over the modelled period lie in the range 188.99 - 200.08 (CAD / t{{CO2}}e) with 95% confidence. This is lower than the deterministic estimate of $222/t {{CO2}}e in the previous post because of the recognition of income tax revenue.

A Scaling Model

The {{siteref("Static_Normals_Model", "Static Normals model")|safe}} (introduced in this post) models the emission of each gas from each NIR sector indepenently. It is therefore possible to extend it to model a strategy for emissions reduction by replacing one of the emissions sectors, and leaving the rest as static normals. This post is about replacing the Static Normals modelling of the Enteric Fermentation sector. Whereas the Static Normals model estimates emissions in the sector with regional [static] normal distributions, the Scaling model, introduced with this post, models Enteric Fermentation emissions instead with unknown variables relating to how Bovaer might influence emissions. The new variables include the following:

  • cattle head-counts, per province and territory, per cattle type
  • per-head methane emission factors, per cattle type
  • fraction of farms administering Bovaer

The assumptions how about these variables are defined, and how they relate to Enteric Fermentation emissions are encoded in new {{siteref("Model_Element", "model elements")|safe}}. The new elements are outlined in Appendix 1, and presented mathematically in Appendix 2. As before, the "Scale Bovaer" strategy implements the idea that some fraction of cattle farmers would be open to starting to administer Bovaer if they were sufficiently incentivized by government subsidy. The model assumes, as before, that a sufficient incentive would be $5000 per year. (All $-denominated values in this site are in CAD unless otherwise indicated.) Differences relative to the previous deterministic model:

  • Whereas the deterministic model fixed the fraction of farmers-open-to-switching-per-year at 5%, the probabilistic model varies it from 2.5-10% uniformly.
  • Whereas the deterministic model fixed production emissions at 45 kg {{CO2e}} / head / year, the probabilistic model varies it uniformly from 20x to 50x less than the reduced methane {{CO2e}}.
  • Whereas the deterministic model ignored income tax, the probabilistic model includes recouping it at a rate of 25% from amounts paid as salaries or subsidies.

Conclusion

This post has introduced a probabilistic "Scaling" model, which is a proof of concept that it is possible to extend a probabilistic model (the Static Normals model) with a strategy, and estimate the impact of the strategy by ablative analysis. The strategy page for "Scale Bovaer" currently features the figures above, and the Bovaer Adoption Limit page [edit: this information is now available via the {{siteref("Model_Explorer", "Model Explorer")|safe}}] describes and shows the assumption that just-over 2.5% to just-under 10% of farmers will start feeding Bovaer per year.

My ambition for PlanZero in the not-too-distant future is to improve the Scaling model iteratively, first by the overall use of an autoregressive baseline model instead of the static normals [edit: this post], and beyond that by sector-by-sector replacements of the generic statistical baselines with bespoke sector models that incorporate additional data sources and strategies. However, before making these iterative improvements I would like to finalize the glossary and perform significant clean-up and refactoring of PlanZero's source code so that those iterative changes will be easier and more systematic [edit: this post].

Until then,

- James Bergstra

Appendix 1: New and Modified Model Elements to Model Bovaer Adoption Probabilistically

The Scaling Model is identical to the {{siteref("Static_Normals_Model", "Static Normals")|safe}} model in every sector except Enteric Fermentation and Other Product Manufacture and Use. This appendix enumerates new {{siteref("Structural_Element", "structural elements")|safe}} in the Scaling model, relative to the Static Normals model. These combine to estimate the changes in those two sectors. The Scaling model totally replaces the estimation of Enteric Fermentation and adds a new term that raises emissions in Other Product Manufacture and Use.

The new structural elements are:

  • Cattle Static Normals
  • Bovaer Adoption Limit
  • Bovaer Production Emissions
  • Bovaer Farm Subsidy
  • Bovaer Purchase Cost
  • Bovaer Monitoring

[edit: The interaction of these elements can be visualized with the new Model Explorer.]

Cattle Population Static Normal

This structural element makes static normal estimates of cattle populations and Enteric Fermentation emission factors to define several unknown variables, including:

  1. head counts
  2. NIR2025 Enteric Fermentation emission factors
  3. NIR2025 overall Enteric Fermentation emissions (including all livestock)
  4. Methane abated through the administration of Bovaer
Estimates are made for each of 8 cattle populations (beef cows, dairy cows, beef heifers for replacement, beef heifers for slaughter, dairy heifers, calves, steers, and bulls), and each province and territory.

The Static Normals model only estimated the overall Enteric Fermentation emission. In this model, this element estimates it instead as a sum-product of the number of cattle of each type, and the emission factors of each type. Details of the probabilistic model are provided below in Appendix 2: Probabilistic Modelling of Cattle Populations and Enteric Fermentation Emission Factors.

Bovaer Adoption Limit

Previously when modelling Bovaer deterministically, this element assumed that no more than 5.0 percent / year of farmers would switch to administering Bovaer in any given year, but that adoption could ultimately rise to 99.0%, (the 1% remainder being organic farmers who can't adopt it).

Here, when used in a probabilistic model, this element assumes instead that 2.5-10.0% of farmers will switch to Bovaer in any given year, and it will vary from year to year within that range.

Bovaer Production Emissions

When used in deterministic models, this element assumes an extra 45.0 kg {{CO2}} / head / year in the Other Product Manufacture and Use sector, in order to account for producing and distributing the Bovaer feed additive.

Here, in the probabilistic model, the amount is based instead on a "20-50x" reduction in CO2e compared with methane emitted from cattle. This was the literal suggestion from an LLM-AI chat earlier this year, which was the original source for the 45.0 kg {{CO2}} / head / year value. For each sampled scenario, a reduction factor is sampled uniformly from 20 to 50. This element does not reflect manufacturer or 3rd party estimates of emission rates nor does it include any change in production emission efficiency over time.

This element also estimates the emission amounts themselves, not just the emission factor. These emissions are added to the emissions of the Other Product Manufacture and Use sector, which is otherwise estimated as it was in the Static Normals model.

Bovaer Farm Subsidy

This element defines the dynamics of farms switching to Bovaer administration, based on (a) the availability of funds in a subsidy program, (b) an assumption of 160 cattle per farm, and (c) a taxable subsidy of $5000 per-farm, per-year. It makes no difference to the model, but in reality I'd imagine the subsidy might well be tied to head counts rather than being a flat rate per farm.

Bovaer Purchase Cost

The Bovaer purchase cost is modelled as being borne directly by the hypothetical government program, at rates of $.20/head/day for calves up to $.50/head/day for bulls and mature cows. The rates were estimated based on this site, and should be modelled with some uncertainty but currently are not.

Bovaer Monitoring

As before, this modelling element assumes administering and monitoring costs 1000.0 CAD / farm / year for paperwork and 3000.0 CAD / farm / year for on-site inspection. When used in probabilistic modelling, this element furthermore estimates that these amounts are paid in labour costs, from which income tax is collected back at a rate of 25%.

Appendix 2: Probabilistic Modelling of Cattle Populations and Enteric Fermentation Emission Factors

The Static Normals model (specifically with Dec 2024 data cutoff) modelled every sector's national and regional (provincial and territorial) emissions in the same way, described in Appendix 2 of the post introducing that model. The Scaling model handles Enteric Emissions differently, in order to introduce variables that could be acted-upon to simulate the effects of scaling Bovaer adoption.

Also, in order to make reasonable inferences of the cattle-type populations and of their respective emission factors, this post draws on more data sources. Statistics Canada publishes national and regional cattle populations for each January 1 and July 1 since the 1930s. That data was described and first used in PlanZero in this post, which explored the causes of Enteric Fermentation emissions. The NIR-2025 document also publishes the emission factors it has used for cattle and other livestock for each year covered, from 1990-2023.

As noted in the previous post on Enteric Fermentation emissions, cattle are responsible for 96-97% of the national total in that sector, with the rest coming from other livestock types. This model assumes they contribute 100%. Future modelling work could compensate for this 3-4% gap in a few ways, but it isn't done yet.

Formally, the Enteric Fermentation emissions, and cattle populations, and emissions factors in the Scaling model are estimated as the posterior distributions in this model after observing data up until Dec 31, 2024 from the three data sources above.
Unknown Variable and Distribution Description
{{latex("\\phi_{(c,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathrm{Lognormal}(-1, 2)")}} Scaled cattle headcount means, by cattle type {{latex("c")}} and region {{latex("r")}}. The scaling factor was the inverse of the largest recorded cattle-type headcount in the historical data, so that the scaled, observed cattle headcounts would lie between 0 and 1.
{{latex("\\beta_{(c,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathrm{Lognormal}(-1, 1)")}} Scaled cattle headcount standard deviations, by cattle type {{latex("c")}} and region {{latex("r")}}.
{{latex("h_{(c,r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\phi_{(c, r)}, \\beta_{(c, r)})")}} Observable scaled cattle headcount, by cattle type {{latex("c")}} and region {{latex("r")}}.
{{latex("\\gamma_{(c)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(A, B^2)")}} A latent emission factor {{latex("c")}} was defined for each cattle type, and used across regions. It was modelled as coming a-priori from a normal distribution around {{latex("A")}}, the approximate NIR-published means over time, and {{latex("B^2")}}, the approximate variance over time.
{{latex("f_{(c)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}(\\gamma_{(c)}, 20)")}} Observable emission factor, defined for each cattle type.
{{latex("\\sigma_{(r)} ~\\sim~ \\mathrm{Lognormal}(-1, 0.7)")}} A-priori, the variance in scaled regional emissions (looking across years) is not expected to be super-tiny, nor much larger than 1.
{{latex("\\sigma_{()} ~\\sim~ \\mathrm{Lognormal}(-1, 0.7)")}} A-priori, the variance in scaled national emissions (looking across years) is not expected to be super-tiny, nor much larger than 1.
{{latex("X_{(r,t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}( \\sum_c h_{(c,r)} f_{(c)}, \\sigma_{(r)}^2)")}} Observable model-estimated scaled NIR emissions for the region ({{latex("r")}}), and annual time period ({{latex("t")}}). Note that the distribution is "static", in that it is the same for all annual time periods.
{{latex("Y_{(t)} ~\\sim~ \\mathcal{N}( \\sum_{r \\in \\mathrm{regions}} \\sum_c h_{(c,r)} f_{(c)}, \\sigma_{(s,g)}^2)")}} Observable model-estimated scaled national-total emissions for Enteric Fermentation emissions (methane). Note that the distribution is static, in that it is the same for all annual time periods.

Posterior estimates of the cattle population and emission factors were obtained using NumPyro's NUTS sampler and drawing 500 samples after warming up for 250. Cattle population counts were adapted for inference by averaging January and July headcounts to get annual headcounts.
